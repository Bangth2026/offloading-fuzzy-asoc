Dear Editor,

Please consider the enclosed manuscript, "Check the Premises Before Imposing the Rules: Auditing Zero-Order TSK Rule Models of Student–AI Interaction", as an original research article for Applied Soft Computing.

Fuzzy rule bases are a natural choice for modelling how students work with large language model assistants: the evidence is graded, and instructors want reasons they can read. The manuscript shows that this readability can hide a failure. A rule model can fit the structure of a course, the propensity of a student or the persistence of a label and still produce rules that sound behavioural. We propose an audit for zero-order Takagi–Sugeno–Kang rule bases with three checks (antecedent signs within cells and within students, the incremental gain over a combined baseline of structure, persistence and propensity, and transfer to a new cohort), each with a pass, fail or inconclusive outcome, and a fixed rule that combines them into one verdict.

The fuzzy contribution is specific. Linear constraints on the consequents make the output provably monotone in the selected directions (Proposition 1), within the literature on monotone TSK identification, and rule support with bootstrap intervals, consequent sign violations and output monotonicity on test data show where a rule table would mislead. In simulations with known processes, compared with a student fixed-effects model, the audit rarely calls a confounded input behavioural and detects moderate within-student effects in longer sessions, including under persistence and under every departure from its assumptions that we tried except a non-monotone effect. On three public datasets it gives three different verdicts: on StudyChat a model of assessment group alone outranks every behavioural model (AUC {{S.grp_p.e}} and {{S.grp_r.e}}) and no behavioural signal remains once that structure is removed; on TutorTrace a rule base for answer-seeking queries passes all three checks in one direction, with the reverse direction and a new task inconclusive; on RECIPE4U the verdict is inconclusive. FURIA, run through Weka, gives inconclusive verdicts in every split. We report these results with equal weight, because the contrast between them is the point of the audit.

We wish to be transparent about related work. An earlier manuscript by the author, submitted to Engineering Applications of Artificial Intelligence (EAAI-26-29396), used StudyChat, the author-specified rule base and a rule-consistency regulariser on a Transformer encoder. That manuscript has been withdrawn and is not under consideration anywhere. The present manuscript shows that the signal it reported is largely assessment-group structure; the overlapping components appear here only as objects of the audit and are summarised in the supplementary material. An earlier draft of the present study was archived with its code on Zenodo (release v1.0.0, 4 November 2026); its behavioural conclusions are withdrawn. All analyses are reported as exploratory: their plans were written and committed before each run, but none was registered with a third party, and Section 3.2 states for which plans the public push can be shown to precede the run.

The code, analysis plans and statistics files are openly available at {{S.repo_url}} and archived on Zenodo (release {{S.release}}: https://doi.org/{{S.vdoi}}). The three datasets are third-party and are not redistributed; RECIPE4U was obtained under its research-use terms and its texts are used only through their lengths, alongside the request labels, ratings and edit flags. No new human-subjects data were collected. I declare no competing interests, and the work received no specific funding.

Reviewers with expertise in fuzzy rule-based modelling, interpretable machine learning or learning analytics would be well placed to evaluate the work.
