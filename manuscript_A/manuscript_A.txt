## Highlights
- Readable fuzzy rules on student–AI logs can encode course structure, not behaviour.
- The audit adds within-cell sign checks, trivial baselines and cohort transfer.
- On StudyChat, a model of assessment group alone outranks every behavioural model.
- In programming, sign-checked TSK rules transfer and beat persistence on a new task.
- In EFL writing, eight rules beat structure but add little to one-line persistence.

## Abstract
Fuzzy rule bases suit models of how students use large language model (LLM) assistants, because the evidence is graded and instructors want readable reasons. Such models can nonetheless encode the structure of a course rather than student behaviour. We propose a three-check audit for fuzzy rule models of interaction data: antecedent signs within structural cells and within students, comparison with trivial baselines (structure, label persistence, student propensity), and cross-cohort transfer under analysis plans fixed in writing before each run. Study 1 audits Takagi–Sugeno–Kang (TSK) models of offloading risk on StudyChat. A learned count-based rule base transfers between semesters (AUC {{S.cf_p}} and {{S.cf_r}}), but a model that knows only the assessment group does better ({{S.grp_p.e}} and {{S.grp_r.e}}), and no within-group association larger than about {{S.eq_maxbound}} can be detected. Studies 2 and 3 target the request itself: whether a programming student asks an AI tutor for the answer (TutorTrace) and whether an EFL student asks ChatGPT to produce text (RECIPE4U). A sign-checked eight-rule TSK classifier transfers in all five splits. In programming its inputs keep their sign within students and it beats label persistence on a new task by {{S.q.S2_D12_to_D3.sc_minus_markov.e}} AUC, but it trails regression on all features. In writing it beats structure by {{S.r1.H2.e}} AUC, but a one-line rule, "the previous request asked for production", comes within {{S.q.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.sc_minus_markov.e}}. We report what each rule base adds over these baselines.

**Keywords:** Takagi–Sugeno–Kang fuzzy rules; Interpretability; Monotone rule bases; Shortcut learning; Learning analytics; Help-seeking; Large language models

# 1. Introduction
Students now complete much of their coursework with LLM assistants beside them. Some use the assistant to repair a misconception; others hand over the reasoning and still submit correct work, a pattern the cognitive-science literature calls cognitive offloading [1]. Field and laboratory studies suggest the second pattern can reduce learning even while assisted performance improves [12,13]. Instructors therefore want an early, readable signal of interactions that look like delegation, and soft computing offers an obvious instrument. The evidence is graded rather than binary, the boundary between productive and delegating use is imprecise, and a fuzzy rule base turns such evidence into a score whose reasons are written in the instructor's own terms [3,4].

A readable model is not thereby a valid one. A rule base encodes premises, for example that unchecked acceptance of generated code signals offloading, or that a student who has written little code before asking wants the answer. When the data carry structure that is correlated with the label (which assessment a record belongs to, how far into a session a request falls, which student wrote it), a model can fit that structure and still produce rules that sound behavioural. The phenomenon is known in machine learning as shortcut learning [20] and, in its evaluation form, as leakage [19]; its simplest statistical form is an aggregation effect of the Simpson type, where a pooled association differs from the associations within groups. Fuzzy rule models are exposed to it in a particular way. Their output is a sentence about behaviour, so a shortcut arrives already explained, and their consequents, fitted cell by cell, can contradict the very direction that justified choosing an input.

We met the problem in our own work. An earlier draft of this paper, built on the public StudyChat corpus [2] and archived with its code on 4 November 2026, reported that a count-based TSK rule base with learned consequents transferred between two semesters. An audit run the same day under a written plan showed that a model knowing only the assessment group outranks every behavioural model on that task, and that within groups none of the behavioural inputs could be shown to be related to the label. This paper reports that audit, turns it into a procedure, and applies the procedure to two further public datasets, TutorTrace [15] in programming and RECIPE4U [16] in EFL writing, where the target is the help request itself rather than a downstream outcome.

The contributions are as follows.

- A three-check audit for fuzzy rule models of interaction data (Section 3), integrated into rule construction rather than applied around it: inputs enter the rule base only if their sign survives within-sample checks, consequents can be constrained to respect that sign, and every model is reported against structure, persistence and propensity baselines and under cross-cohort transfer. The procedure and the two constrained constructions are stated as Algorithms 1–3. The individual ingredients are standard; what is new is their combination for rule models whose text can disguise a shortcut, together with interpretability measures (rule support, monotonicity violations) that make the disguise visible.
- Study 1 (StudyChat): the "assisted minus unaided" proxy for offloading risk is dominated by assessment structure, the apparent sign reversal of an expert rule is a between-group effect, and a learned rule base that passed a planned cross-semester test carries little within-group signal.
- Study 2 (TutorTrace): a sign-checked TSK classifier for answer-seeking queries transfers across deployments and to a new task. Its inputs keep their sign within students, and on the new task it beats label persistence and student propensity by about 0.2 AUC. It does not clearly exceed session structure on the original task, and it trails a regression on all behavioural features.
- Study 3 (RECIPE4U): the same classifier for production requests transfers across writing courses and beats structure, but most of its signal is persistence of the student's own earlier requests. A one-line rule nearly matches it, and without the lagged-label input it falls to {{S.q.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.nolag.e}}.

Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 states the audit and the rule models, Sections 4–6 report the three studies, Section 7 reports interpretability measures, Section 8 discusses the implications and Section 9 lists the limitations.

# 2. Related work
## 2.1 Fuzzy systems in learner modelling
Fuzzy logic has a long record in adaptive and intelligent tutoring, where knowledge levels, misconceptions and engagement are graded quantities. Chrysafiadi and Virvou [9] used fuzzy sets and rules to adapt instruction in an e-learning environment for programming, and much later work follows the same pattern: expert-defined linguistic terms over learner indicators, with rules that map them to an adaptation decision. Rule-constrained neural learners are a newer branch. Injecting logical rules into network training through soft constraints [23] or differentiable fuzzy operators [18] lets a model keep the rules' readability while learning from data, and recent learner models inject educational knowledge into knowledge-tracing networks with the same aim [24]. These approaches assume that the premises of the rules hold for the population at hand, which is the assumption our audit tests.

## 2.2 Interpretability and accuracy of fuzzy rule bases
The trade-off between interpretability and accuracy is a central theme of fuzzy modelling. Gacto et al. [8] review interpretability measures for linguistic rule bases, separating complexity (number of rules, antecedents per rule, terms per input) from semantic properties such as distinguishable terms and consistent rules. Data-driven rule generation ranges from the one-pass Wang–Mendel method [6] to ANFIS, which tunes memberships and consequents by gradient descent [5,22], and to rule induction such as FURIA [7]. Outside fuzzy logic, generalised additive models with pairwise interactions [10], implemented as Explainable Boosting Machines [11], offer a strong interpretable baseline. These methods differ in how they trade readability for fit, but none of them guards against fitting a structural confound. Section 7 reports the measures of [8] for every learned fuzzy model in this paper, adding two that matter for the audit: the number of training cases supporting each rule and the number of consequent pairs that contradict the sign of their input.

## 2.3 Help-seeking in intelligent tutoring
Studies 2 and 3 sit closer to the help-seeking literature than to the offloading literature. Aleven et al. [25] review help seeking in interactive learning environments and distinguish productive requests from requests that bypass learning; their model of help seeking with a Cognitive Tutor [26] encoded desired and undesired help-seeking behaviour as production rules evaluated against student actions. Baker and colleagues showed that "gaming the system", including rapid help requests that extract answers, can be detected from interaction logs and is associated with lower learning [27,28]. A dependent query in TutorTrace and a production request in RECIPE4U are LLM-era forms of answer-seeking help. We use the help-seeking construct for those two studies and treat the link to learning as an open question rather than a premise.

## 2.4 Student–LLM interaction data
Bastani et al. [12] show experimentally that unrestricted generative-AI access in high-school mathematics improves assisted practice but harms later unaided performance, and Fan et al. [13] report a related loss of self-regulation. Large logs of real student–LLM dialogue have only recently become public. StudyChat [2] contains student–ChatGPT dialogues from two semesters of a university AI course, annotated with dialogue acts and linked to assignment and exam scores. Hasan and Jabe [14] report that dialogue-act features from StudyChat do not add out-of-sample predictive value for exam scores beyond prior performance; Study 1 reaches a compatible conclusion by locating the apparent signal in assessment structure. TutorTrace [15] logs fine-grained IDE activity around AI-tutor queries in introductory programming, with queries labelled as guided or dependent help-seeking. RECIPE4U [16] logs student–ChatGPT exchanges in EFL writing courses, with every student utterance labelled for intent.

## 2.5 Shortcuts, leakage and analysis plans
Geirhos et al. [20] describe shortcut learning as solving a benchmark through cues that do not transfer to the intended task. Kapoor and Narayanan [19] catalogue leakage in machine-learning-based science, including evaluation designs that let structure in the data inflate performance. Pre-registration [21] separates confirmatory from exploratory analysis by fixing the plan before the data are examined. Our analysis plans were written before each run and committed to a public repository, but none was registered with a third party; Section 3.2 states exactly what can be verified.

# 3. The audit and the rule models
## 3.1 Three checks
Figure 1 summarises the audit and Algorithm 1 states it step by step. It applies to any rule model whose antecedents are computed from interaction data and whose label may be correlated with structure in that data.

!FIG figs/fig1_audit.png | Figure 1. The three-check audit and its outcome in Studies 1–3 (S1–S3).

**Check 1: antecedent signs within cells and within students.** For each input, estimate its association with the label within each structural cell (assessment group, deployment, course) and within each student, by rank correlation of within-cell or within-student ranks, with an interval clustered by student. A rule whose sign holds in pooled data but not within cells encodes structure; a rule whose sign holds across students but not within them describes students rather than requests. In rule construction the check takes the form of a split-half sign test on the training data (Section 3.3).

**Check 2: trivial baselines.** Fit the same protocol with models that use no behaviour: one that sees only structural variables, one that predicts the label of a request from the label of the previous request in the same session (persistence), and one that uses the student's earlier share of positive labels (propensity). A behavioural rule model is informative to the extent that it improves on these, and the improvement is the quantity to report.

**Check 3: transfer under a written plan.** Train on one cohort and score another once, with hypotheses, decision rules and margins fixed in writing before the run.

!CODE
Algorithm 1. Audit of a rule model f on interaction data
Input : training cohort D_tr, test cohort D_te; label y; behavioural inputs X;
        structural cells G; student identifier u; written plan P (hypotheses,
        margins, interval levels)
Output: within-sample associations, baseline gaps, transfer decisions
 1  push P to a public host before f or any baseline touches D_tr or D_te
    // Check 1: antecedent signs
 2  for each input x used by f, with rule direction d_x in {-1, +1} do
 3      rho_G(x) <- Spearman correlation of within-cell ranks of x and y
 4      rho_u(x) <- Spearman correlation of within-student ranks of x and y
 5      interval of each rho: student-cluster bootstrap, B = 2000
 6      if the interval excludes 0 with sign d_x then  x is supported
 7      else if it excludes 0 with sign -d_x then     x contradicts its rule
 8      else                                           x is not supported
 9  end for
    // Check 2: trivial baselines (only b_S has parameters, fitted on D_tr)
10  b_S <- logistic regression on structural variables
11  b_M <- 1 if the previous request of the session had y = 1, 0 if not, 0.5 if none
12  b_P <- the student's share of y = 1 among earlier requests, 0.5 if none
13  for each b in {b_S, b_M, b_P}: Delta_b <- AUC(f) - AUC(b) on D_te, with interval
    // Check 3: transfer under P
14  fit f once on D_tr; score D_te once; decide every hypothesis of P at its
    Bonferroni-corrected level
15  return rho_G, rho_u, Delta, decisions
    // a rule is read as behavioural only if its inputs are supported in
    // lines 6-8 and the lower bounds of Delta in line 13 exceed 0
!END
!CAP Algorithm 1. The audit. Lines 3 and 4 are the two halves of Check 1; in Study 1 the cells are semester × assessment group, in Studies 2 and 3 the student is the cell.

## 3.3 TSK rule bases
All rule bases are zero-order TSK systems [4]. Each input x has two linguistic terms with ramp memberships, HIGH(x) = clip((x − a)/(b − a), 0, 1) and LOW(x) = 1 − HIGH(x), where a and b are the 25th and 75th percentiles of the training data. When a = b (for example, when most training values are zero) b is set to a + 10⁻³, which makes the membership a step at a; Section 7 lists where this happens. With three inputs the full grid gives eight rules combined by the product t-norm, and the output is

!EQ s = (Σ_j w_j z_j + w_0 z_0) / (Σ_j w_j + w_0),

with w_j the firing degree of rule j, z_j ∈ [0, 1] its consequent and a background term w_0 = 0.05, z_0 = 0.5 that keeps s defined when no rule fires.

The **expert rule base** of Study 1 (FR1–FR5) reads four unit-level shares: v, the share of writing requests not followed by an editing or verification turn; u, the share of turns in runs of three or more assignment-specific questions; p, the share of provide-context turns; and e, the share of verification turns. Its consequents are fixed by the author (FR1: v HIGH → risk 1.0; FR4: v LOW and e HIGH → risk 0.0; and so on). These rules were written by the author from the source study, not elicited from a panel of instructors.

The **learned rule bases** fit the consequents. Because s is linear in z once the firing degrees are known, z solves the quadratic programme

!EQ z = argmin_{0 ≤ z ≤ 1} ‖A z + b − y‖² + α ‖z − π‖²,

with A_ij = w_ij / (Σ_k w_ik + w_0), b_i = w_0 z_0 / (Σ_k w_ik + w_0), π the training base rate and α = 1. Each z_j is close to the proportion of positive cases among units that strongly match rule j, pulled toward the base rate when few units match it. In Study 1 the inputs are log counts of dialogue acts (CF-TSK).

**SC-TSK** (Studies 2 and 3) adds Check 1 to construction. Candidate behavioural inputs are ranked by |AUC − 0.5| on the training data; an input is eligible only if its direction (AUC above or below 0.5) is the same in two halves of the training students. The top three eligible inputs form the grid (Algorithm 2). **SM-TSK**, added in revision, also makes the consequents respect those directions: for every pair of rules that differ only in input i, with selected direction d_i ∈ {−1, +1}, the constraint d_i (z_HIGH − z_LOW) ≥ 0 is added to the programme above. The constraints are linear, so the problem remains a small quadratic programme (eight variables, twelve constraints), solved here by SLSQP from the SC-TSK solution. Sensitivity to w_0 ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.2} and α ∈ {0.1, 1, 10} is reported in Sections 5–6.

!CODE
Algorithm 2. SC-TSK: sign-checked construction of a zero-order TSK rule base
Input : training requests (x_n, y_n, u_n), n = 1..N; candidate inputs c_1..c_m;
        k = 3; w_0 = 0.05; z_0 = 0.5; alpha = 1; seed 0
Output: selected inputs S, directions d, memberships, consequents z
 1  x <- sign(x) * log(1 + |x|) for every candidate
 2  split the training students at random into halves H_1, H_2
 3  for each candidate c with at least two distinct values do
 4      a_c <- AUC(c, y);  a_c1, a_c2 <- AUC on H_1, H_2 (0.5 if a half has one class)
 5      eligible(c) <- sign(a_c1 - 0.5) = sign(a_c2 - 0.5) and a_c1 != 0.5
 6      d_c <- sign(a_c - 0.5)
 7  end for
 8  S <- the k eligible candidates with the largest |a_c - 0.5|
 9  for each i in S do
10      q_i, Q_i <- 25th and 75th training percentiles; if Q_i - q_i < 1e-9 then Q_i <- q_i + 1e-3
11      HIGH_i(x) <- clip((x - q_i) / (Q_i - q_i), 0, 1);  LOW_i(x) <- 1 - HIGH_i(x)
12  end for
13  rules R_1..R_8: every LOW/HIGH combination over S; w_j(x) <- product of its memberships
14  A_nj <- w_j(x_n) / (sum_l w_l(x_n) + w_0);  b_n <- w_0 z_0 / (sum_l w_l(x_n) + w_0)
15  z <- argmin over [0,1]^8 of ||A z + b - y||^2 + alpha ||z - pi||^2   (bounded least squares)
16  return S, d, (q, Q), z;  score s(x) = (sum_j w_j(x) z_j + w_0 z_0) / (sum_j w_j(x) + w_0)
!END
!CAP Algorithm 2. SC-TSK. Line 5 is Check 1 inside construction; π is the training base rate.

!CODE
Algorithm 3. SM-TSK: monotone consequents
Input : A, b, y, pi, alpha from Algorithm 2; directions d_i, i in S; SC-TSK solution z^0
Output: consequents z that respect d
 1  C <- empty set
 2  for each i in S and each rule j whose term on input i is LOW do
 3      k <- the rule equal to j except HIGH on input i
 4      add the constraint d_i (z_k - z_j) >= 0 to C        // |C| = 3 x 4 = 12
 5  end for
 6  z <- argmin over [0,1]^8 of ||A z + b - y||^2 + alpha ||z - pi||^2 subject to C
         (SLSQP started at clip(z^0, 0, 1); at most 500 iterations; tolerance 1e-10)
 7  violations(z) <- number of (j, k, d_i) in C with d_i (z_k - z_j) < -1e-6
 8  return z
!END
!CAP Algorithm 3. SM-TSK. Line 7, applied to z^0, gives the sign violations of SC-TSK in Table 5.

Algorithm 2 costs O(m N log N) for the AUCs and a bounded least-squares problem in eight variables; Algorithm 3 adds twelve linear constraints to the same problem. On synthetic data of the size used here (600 requests, nine candidates) Algorithm 2 took at most 0.07 s and Algorithm 3 at most 0.05 s over 20 repetitions, so the two checks add no practical cost to building a rule base.

For rule injection into neural encoders (Study 1 only) we read rule j as "if the antecedent holds, risk is about z_j" and penalise violations of the Łukasiewicz bounds w_j z_j ≤ r ≤ 1 − w_j(1 − z_j) with a hinge loss. A rule that fires fully pins r to z_j; when several rules fire fully with different consequents the bounds conflict, and the loss settles where the summed violation is smallest. Every encoder is also trained with binary cross-entropy, so the constraint is never satisfied exactly.

Intervals are 95% student-cluster bootstrap intervals (2,000 resamples) unless a hypothesis test with Bonferroni correction is stated, in which case the corrected level is given (98.75% for the four hypotheses of E10, 98.33% for the three of Studies 2 and 3). Settings of all comparators are listed in Appendix A.

# 4. Study 1: offloading risk on StudyChat
## 4.1 Data and label
StudyChat [2] holds student–ChatGPT dialogues from two offerings of a university AI course (Fall 2024, Spring 2025), each turn labelled by GPT-4.1 with one of eight broad and 31 specific dialogue acts. Assignments 1–2 precede Exam 1, assignments 3–4 precede Exam 2, and assignments 5–7 precede the final exam; exams are taken without AI access. We form one unit per student and assessment group and define the weak label as the relative gap between the assisted assignment average and the unaided exam,

!EQ y = clip((llm_g − no_llm_g) / llm_g, 0, 1),

marking the top quartile as high risk. The quartile is computed on all values of y, including the {{S.s1_zero_share}}% that are clipped to zero (pooled threshold {{S.s1_thr}}). This gives {{S.n_units}} units from {{S.n_students}} students.

## 4.2 What the analyses showed before the audit

## 4.3 The audit
**Check 2 fails first.** The last row of Table 2 is a regression that knows only which assessment group a unit belongs to. It reaches {{S.grp_p.e}} (CI {{S.grp_p.lo}} to {{S.grp_p.hi}}) from Fall to Spring, {{S.grp_r.e}} (CI {{S.grp_r.lo}} to {{S.grp_r.hi}}) from Spring to Fall and {{S.grp_cv}} in pooled cross-validation, above every behavioural model. Figure 2a shows why. With the Fall 2024 threshold, {{S.s1cells.f24g1.pos_f24_threshold}} of {{S.s1cells.f24g1.n}} units are high-risk in group 1 of Fall 2024, {{S.s1cells.f24g2.pos_f24_threshold}} of {{S.s1cells.f24g2.n}} in group 2 and {{S.s1cells.f24g3.pos_f24_threshold}} of {{S.s1cells.f24g3.n}} in group 3; in Spring 2025 the counts are {{S.s1cells.s25g1.pos_f24_threshold}}/{{S.s1cells.s25g1.n}}, {{S.s1cells.s25g2.pos_f24_threshold}}/{{S.s1cells.s25g2.n}} and {{S.s1cells.s25g3.pos_f24_threshold}}/{{S.s1cells.s25g3.n}}. In Fall 2024 group 2, {{S.s1cells.f24g2.zeros}} of {{S.s1cells.f24g2.n}} units have y = 0 because the exam score exceeds the assignment average. # 5. Study 2: answer-seeking queries on TutorTrace
## 5.1 Data, target and protocol
TutorTrace [15] records millisecond IDE telemetry from introductory Python sessions with an integrated AI tutor across four classroom deployments. Each AI query is labelled guided (asks for a hint or explanation) or dependent (asks for the answer or the code); labels come from GPT-4o and were validated against two human raters for deployments 1 and 2 (κ = .709 and .690, substantial agreement in the terms of Landis and Koch [29]). We use the released query-level metrics, which summarise activity since the previous query (code edits, characters inserted and deleted, terminal runs and errors, failed tests, idle and reading times). Chat text and code are not used. Deployment 1 (D1) contributes {{S.q.S2_D1_to_D2.n_train}} labelled queries ({{S.q.S2_D1_to_D2.pos_train}} dependent) from {{S.tt_counts['1'].students}} students, D2 {{S.q.S2_D1_to_D2.n_test}} ({{S.q.S2_D1_to_D2.pos_test}} dependent) from {{S.tt_counts['2'].students}}, and D3, a different task with unvalidated labels, {{S.q.S2_D12_to_D3.n_test}} ({{S.q.S2_D12_to_D3.pos_test}} dependent) from {{S.tt_counts['3'].students}}.

The plan fixed three hypotheses for D1 → D2 at Bonferroni α = 0.0167: transfer (H1, lower bound of AUC above 0.5), behaviour beyond structure (H2, lower bound of the AUC gain of structure + SC-TSK over structure alone above 0), and non-inferiority to a logistic regression on all behavioural features (H3, margin 0.05). Structure is the query's position in the session, whether it is the first query, its time since session start and the test progress at the moment of asking; all four mean the same in every deployment. The length of the query text was excluded from the main analysis because the label is assigned from that text; Study 3 admits lagged labels of earlier requests, for reasons given in Section 6.1.

# 6. Study 3: production requests in EFL writing (RECIPE4U)
## 6.1 Data, target and protocol
RECIPE4U [16] contains student–ChatGPT exchanges from three EFL writing courses at one university, collected over a semester as students revised essays with ChatGPT. Every student utterance carries an intent label. We obtained the data through the authors' consent form, use utterance and essay texts only through their lengths, and redistribute nothing. Among request utterances we set y = 1 for production requests, which ask ChatGPT to generate, revise or translate the student's text, and y = 0 for guidance requests, which ask for information, evaluation, language-use advice or confirmation. The Scientific Writing course (SW) contributes {{S.rc_fmt.SW.n}} requests ({{S.q.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.pos_train}} production) from {{S.rc_fmt.SW.s}} students; Academic Writing (AW) and Intermediate Reading and Writing (IRW) together contribute {{S.q.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.n_test}} ({{S.q.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.pos_test}} production) from {{S.rc_fmt.AW.s}} and {{S.rc_fmt.IRW.s}} students.

The plan copies Study 2 with features computed from earlier exchanges of the same session: number of earlier turns, share of earlier requests that were production requests, earlier essay edits, mean earlier satisfaction rating, mean lengths of earlier student utterances and ChatGPT replies, and essay length and growth before the request. Structure is course, week, session and position in the session. Transfer is tested across courses: train on SW and test on AW and IRW together (primary), and the reverse. Unlike the text length excluded in Study 2, the share of earlier production requests uses labels of earlier requests only, which an instructor-facing system would have observed by the time of the next request. It does require an intent classifier in deployment, whose errors would propagate.

# 8. Discussion
The three studies answer the question in the title differently. In StudyChat the target was a downstream outcome tied to assessment structure, and every rule model, expert or learned, fuzzy or not, found the structure. The planned cross-semester test of E10 did not protect against this, because the structure itself transfers: each semester has the same three assessment groups with similar relative difficulty. In TutorTrace and RECIPE4U the target was the request, and the same sign-checked rule base transferred in all five splits, but the audit gives the two results different meanings. In programming, the inputs carry within-student signal and the rule base beats persistence and propensity on a new task, while session structure already explains much of what it predicts on the original task. In writing, structure explains nothing, but a student's own previous request explains nearly everything the rule base predicts. The audit did not make any fuzzy model better. It showed what each had learned, and that should decide whether its rules are shown to an instructor.

Why should the audit matter more for fuzzy rule models than for other model families, given that Study 1 shows the same failure in all of them? Two reasons follow from the results. First, a rule base presents its shortcut as a behavioural sentence. A regression on assessment-group indicators announces what it uses; an eight-rule table about writing requests and conceptual questions does not, even when its predictions are driven by group structure. Second, consequents fitted cell by cell can contradict the directions that justified the inputs (Table 5), so a rule base can be internally inconsistent while each rule reads plausibly. Checks 1 and 2 address the first problem and the monotone programme addresses the second. Both fit naturally inside fuzzy rule construction, as a selection rule for antecedents and as linear constraints on consequents.

Three practical lessons follow. An antecedent's sign should be checked within the cells of whatever structure the data carry and within students, on the training data, before the rule is trusted or imposed as a constraint; in Study 1 that check would have shown at once that FR1 had no within-group support. Trivial baselines (structure, persistence, propensity) belong in every results table; they changed the conclusion of Study 1 and the interpretation of Study 3. And interpretability has a measurable price that should be reported: three inputs cost between about 0.01 and 0.10 AUC against a regression on all features in programming and nothing in writing, and monotone consequents cost little except in one split.

Several alternative explanations bound the substantive reading. In StudyChat, students with few logged interactions may have used other AI tools, so low logged volume need not mean low use, and prior ability correlates with both exam scores and interaction style. In TutorTrace, the guided/dependent label is model-generated, and a dependent request may be a reasonable choice for a student who is stuck. In RECIPE4U, persistence predicts when the next production request comes but says nothing about why a student delegates, and a production request may be followed by careful revision. We did not evaluate the rule tables with instructors, so statements about their usefulness are hypotheses: the tables are short and state their conditions, but whether instructors find them plausible, actionable or faithful to the model's behaviour remains to be tested.

# 9. Limitations
- Study 1 uses one course at one university; the structural confound is specific to its assessment design, though the check that found it is not. Its expert rules were written by the author from the source study rather than elicited from instructors.
- Most analysis plans carry only local commit timestamps (Section 3.2). Analyses from E7 onward were designed after earlier results on the same corpus, and Study 3's plan was written after Study 2's results were known.
- Studies 2 and 3 use one institution each. Study 2's sessions are 10–15 minutes long and its labels are model-generated; those of D3 were not validated. Study 3's grouping of intents into production and guidance requests is ours.
- TutorTrace's licence is stated as CC BY 4.0 in its metadata and marked as pending confirmation in its datasheet; we use only released numeric metrics and labels, redistribute nothing, and have asked the maintainers to confirm.
- None of the studies measures learning. Study 1's outcome is a noisy proxy, and the targets of Studies 2 and 3 are help-seeking acts. Linking these patterns to learning requires data with unaided outcomes and comparison conditions, such as a randomised classroom study [17], whose data we have requested.
- The rule tables were not evaluated with users.

# 10. Conclusion
Fuzzy rule bases make models of student–AI interaction readable, and readability makes them persuasive. We showed that this persuasiveness can hide a model that has learned the structure of a course or the persistence of a label rather than the behaviour it describes, and proposed an audit that exposes both: antecedent signs within cells and within students, trivial baselines, and transfer under written plans, with monotone consequents to keep the rule text consistent with the evidence that selected it. On StudyChat the audit overturned our own earlier result. On TutorTrace it confirmed a transferable rule base for answer-seeking queries whose inputs carry within-student signal, and made its cost in accuracy explicit. On RECIPE4U it showed that an eight-rule base which beats structure adds little to a one-line persistence rule. We suggest that fuzzy models of learning data report these checks as routinely as they report accuracy.

# Appendix A. Settings of all models
All logistic regressions: inputs standardised, L2 penalty with C = 1, lbfgs solver, max_iter = 2000, no tuning (an unpenalised fit, C = 10⁶, is used only for the prior-ability adjustment of Section 4.4). Decision trees: CART with depth 2 (and 3 in Study 1), min_samples_leaf = 10, random_state = 0. Wang–Mendel: the same LOW/HIGH ramps as the TSK models; each training case generates the rule of its maximal firing degree; a rule's consequent is the firing-weighted mean label of the cases that generated it; unoccupied rules are dropped. ANFIS-style TSK: eight rules, sigmoid memberships initialised at the quartile ramps (centre (a + b)/2, slope 4/(b − a)), consequents through a sigmoid initialised at the training base rate, memberships and consequents trained jointly by Adam (learning rate 0.01, 300 full-batch epochs, binary cross-entropy), predictions averaged over seeds 0–9. EBM: interpret 0.7.8, ExplainableBoostingClassifier with default settings and random_state = 0. Sequence encoders of Study 1: Transformer with two layers, two heads, model width 32 and pooling through an assessment token; LSTM with one layer of width 32; both trained for 15 epochs with Adam (10⁻³) and batch 32. No hyperparameter was tuned on held-out data. When fewer than three candidate inputs passed the sign check in an R2 refit, the best-ranked remaining candidate was added; the three cases are listed in results/r2_stats.json.

# Appendix B. Confirmatory tests on Spring 2025
Seven tests used Spring 2025 as the held-out semester: E10 H1 (CF-TSK non-inferior to LR8), H2 (CF-TSK above chance), H3 (rule constraint improves a Transformer) and H4 (fusion of CF-TSK and LR8 improves on LR8); E11 H1 and H2 (as E10 H1 and H2 with nested input selection); and E12 H5 (rule constraint improves an LSTM). Under Bonferroni correction at 0.05/7, all decisions are unchanged: E10 H1, H2, H4, E11 H1, H2 and E12 H5 hold, and E10 H3 does not. E12 H5 holds with a mean gain of about 0.001 AUC, which Section 4.4 shows to be negligible.
