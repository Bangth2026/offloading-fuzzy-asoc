## Highlights
- Readable fuzzy rules on student–AI logs can encode course structure, not behaviour.
- An audit reports within-student signs, trivial baselines and transfer separately.
- Monotone consequents make the TSK output provably monotone in each selected input.
- In programming, rule inputs keep their sign within students but fail Check 2.
- In EFL writing, an eight-rule base adds little to a one-line persistence rule.

## Abstract
Fuzzy rule bases suit models of how students use large language model (LLM) assistants, because the evidence is graded and instructors want readable reasons. Such models can nonetheless encode the structure of a course rather than student behaviour. We propose a three-check audit for fuzzy rule models of interaction data, reported check by check: antecedent signs within structural cells and within students, comparison with trivial baselines (structure, label persistence, student propensity), and cross-cohort transfer under written analysis plans. For Takagi–Sugeno–Kang (TSK) rule bases, linear constraints make the consequents, and provably the output, monotone in the selected directions, and three measures (rule support, consequent sign violations, output monotonicity on test data) show where a rule table would mislead. In simulations with known processes, naive transfer accepts structural and propensity shortcuts, while the audit rarely does; it detects within-student effects under these confounds when they are large enough, but is conservative when persistence dominates. On StudyChat, a model of assessment group alone ranks student–assessment units better than a TSK model of offloading risk ({{S.grp_p.e}} and {{S.grp_r.e}} against {{S.cf_p}} and {{S.cf_r}}). On TutorTrace, the inputs of an eight-rule classifier of answer-seeking queries keep their sign within students, but the rule base does not reliably beat all baselines in any deployment. On RECIPE4U, a one-line persistence rule comes within {{S.q.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.sc_minus_markov.e}} AUC of the rule base. No rule base passes all three checks. All analyses are exploratory.

**Keywords:** Takagi–Sugeno–Kang fuzzy rules; Interpretability; Monotone rule bases; Shortcut learning; Learning analytics; Help-seeking; Large language models

# 1. Introduction
Students now complete much of their coursework with LLM assistants beside them. Some use the assistant to repair a misconception; others hand over the reasoning and still submit correct work, a pattern the cognitive-science literature calls cognitive offloading [1]. Field and laboratory studies suggest the second pattern can reduce learning even while assisted performance improves [12,13]. Instructors therefore want an early, readable signal of interactions that look like delegation, and soft computing offers an obvious instrument. The evidence is graded rather than binary, the boundary between productive and delegating use is imprecise, and a fuzzy rule base turns such evidence into a score whose reasons are written in the instructor's own terms [3,4].

A readable model is not thereby a valid one. A rule base encodes premises, for example that unchecked acceptance of generated code signals offloading, or that a student who has written little code before asking wants the answer. When the data carry structure that is correlated with the label (which assessment a record belongs to, how far into a session a request falls, which student wrote it), a model can fit that structure and still produce rules that sound behavioural. The phenomenon is known in machine learning as shortcut learning [20] and, in its evaluation form, as leakage [19]; its simplest statistical form is an aggregation effect of the Simpson type, where a pooled association differs from the associations within groups [37]. Fuzzy rule models are exposed to it in a particular way. Their output is a sentence about behaviour, so a shortcut arrives already explained, and their consequents, each estimated mainly from the cases its rule covers, can contradict the very direction that justified choosing an input.

This paper turns these observations into an audit and applies it to three public datasets. On StudyChat [2], a count-based TSK model of offloading risk passes a planned cross-semester transfer test, yet a model that knows only the assessment group ranks student–assessment units better. On TutorTrace [15] in programming and RECIPE4U [16] in EFL writing, the target is the help request itself rather than a downstream outcome, and the audit separates what a rule base adds over structure, label persistence and student propensity.

The contributions are as follows.

- A three-check audit for fuzzy rule models of interaction data (Section 3, Algorithm 1): antecedent signs within structural cells and within students, trivial baselines, and cross-cohort transfer under a written plan, with a verdict reported for each check. Its ingredients are established practice in learning analytics and in the leakage literature [19,20,37]; what we add is their application to rule models, whose text can present a shortcut as a behavioural statement.
- Measures and a construction specific to fuzzy rule bases (Sections 3.3 and 8): the support of each rule, sign violations between consequent pairs, and the share of test cases where the output moves against the direction of its input. SM-TSK removes the violations by linear constraints on the consequents (Algorithm 3); Proposition 1 states that this makes the output monotone, a known type of condition [31,33] that we prove for the grid used here.
- A test of the audit on simulated data with known generating processes, including mixtures of a true within-student effect with a structural, propensity or persistence confound at several effect sizes and session lengths (Section 7).
- Three case studies. On StudyChat (Study 1) the "assisted minus unaided" proxy for offloading risk is dominated by assessment structure, and the apparent sign reversal of an author-specified rule is a between-group effect. On TutorTrace (Study 2) the inputs of a classifier for answer-seeking queries pass Check 1, but the rule base fails Check 2. On RECIPE4U (Study 3) the rule base adds little to a one-line persistence rule.

The author-specified rule base of Study 1 and a rule-consistency regulariser for a Transformer on StudyChat were also used in an earlier manuscript by the author, which has been withdrawn; here they appear only as objects of the audit (Supplementary S1). All analyses are exploratory. Their plans were written before each run and are listed with what can be verified about their timing (Section 3.2). Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 states the audit and the rule models, Sections 4–6 report the three studies, Section 7 the simulation, Section 8 interpretability measures, calibration and variants of the rule base, Section 9 the discussion and Section 10 the limitations.

# 2. Related work
## 2.1 Fuzzy systems in learner modelling
Fuzzy logic has a long record in adaptive and intelligent tutoring, where knowledge levels, misconceptions and engagement are graded quantities. Chrysafiadi and Virvou [9] used fuzzy sets and rules to adapt instruction in an e-learning environment for programming, and much later work follows the same pattern: expert-defined linguistic terms over learner indicators, with rules that map them to an adaptation decision. Rule-constrained neural learners are a newer branch. Injecting logical rules into network training through soft constraints [23] or differentiable fuzzy operators [18] lets a model keep the rules' readability while learning from data, and recent learner models inject educational knowledge into knowledge-tracing networks with the same aim [24]. These approaches assume that the premises of the rules hold for the population at hand, which is the assumption our audit tests.

## 2.2 Interpretability and accuracy of fuzzy rule bases
The trade-off between interpretability and accuracy is a central theme of fuzzy modelling. Gacto et al. [8] review interpretability measures for linguistic rule bases, separating complexity (number of rules, antecedents per rule, terms per input) from semantic properties such as distinguishable terms and consistent rules. Data-driven rule generation ranges from the one-pass Wang–Mendel method [6] to ANFIS, which tunes memberships and consequents by gradient descent [5,22], and to rule induction such as FURIA [7]. Outside fuzzy logic, generalised additive models with pairwise interactions [10], implemented as Explainable Boosting Machines [11], offer a strong interpretable baseline. These methods differ in how they trade readability for fit, but none of them guards against fitting a structural confound.

## 2.3 Monotone and constraint-based fuzzy models
Monotonicity is the semantic property closest to the audit, because a rule whose consequent moves against the direction of its antecedent is exactly the inconsistency an instructor cannot see. Won et al. [31] derived parameter conditions under which a TSK system is monotone. For zero-order TSK systems, Tay, Lim and co-workers studied sufficient conditions and monotonicity tests, built monotone systems by constrained identification from data [33], and restored monotonicity by relabelling [32] or interpolating [34] rules; Van Broekhoven and De Baets [35] characterised monotone Mamdani–Assilian models. Outside fuzzy logic, shape-constrained additive models impose monotone smooth terms [36]. In all of this work the direction of monotonicity is given by domain knowledge. SM-TSK is an instance of constrained identification of a zero-order TSK system; what it adds is the source of the directions, which come from a within-sample sign check on the training data, and the use of the constraints as part of an audit rather than as prior knowledge. Section 3.3 states the monotonicity guarantee for the grid used here.

## 2.4 Help-seeking in intelligent tutoring
Studies 2 and 3 sit closer to the help-seeking literature than to the offloading literature. Aleven et al. [25] review help seeking in interactive learning environments and distinguish productive requests from requests that bypass learning; their model of help seeking with a Cognitive Tutor [26] encoded desired and undesired help-seeking behaviour as production rules evaluated against student actions. Baker and colleagues showed that "gaming the system", including rapid help requests that extract answers, can be detected from interaction logs and is associated with lower learning [27,28]. A dependent query in TutorTrace and a production request in RECIPE4U are LLM-era forms of answer-seeking help. We use the help-seeking construct for those two studies and treat the link to learning as an open question rather than a premise.

## 2.5 Student–LLM interaction data
Bastani et al. [12] show experimentally that unrestricted generative-AI access in high-school mathematics improves assisted practice but harms later unaided performance, and Fan et al. [13] report a related loss of self-regulation. Large logs of real student–LLM dialogue have only recently become public. StudyChat [2] contains student–ChatGPT dialogues from two semesters of a university AI course, annotated with dialogue acts and linked to assignment and exam scores. A recent preprint reports that dialogue-act features from StudyChat do not add out-of-sample predictive value for exam scores beyond prior performance [14]; Study 1 is consistent with this. TutorTrace [15] logs fine-grained IDE activity around AI-tutor queries in introductory programming, with queries labelled as guided or dependent help-seeking. RECIPE4U [16] logs student–ChatGPT exchanges in EFL writing courses, with every student utterance labelled for intent.

## 2.6 Shortcuts, aggregation and analysis plans
Geirhos et al. [20] describe shortcut learning as solving a benchmark through cues that do not transfer to the intended task. Kapoor and Narayanan [19] catalogue leakage in machine-learning-based science, including evaluation designs that let structure in the data inflate performance. Kievit et al. [37] show how pooled and within-group associations can differ in sign and give practical checks for the difference; Check 1 applies such a check to rule antecedents. Pre-registration [21] separates confirmatory from exploratory analysis by fixing the plan before the data are examined. Our plans were written before each run, but none was registered with a third party, and several were written after earlier results on the same data; we therefore treat all analyses as exploratory.

# 3. The audit and the rule models
## 3.1 Three checks
Figure 1 summarises the audit and Algorithm 1 states it step by step. It applies to any rule model whose antecedents are computed from interaction data and whose label may be correlated with structure in that data.

!FIG figs/fig1_audit.png | Figure 1. The three-check audit. Outcomes: Check 1 fails for the author-specified rule in Study 1 (FR1 has no within-group sign), passes for all inputs in Study 2 and only for the lagged-label input in Study 3; Check 2 fails in all three studies (structure in Study 1; persistence or propensity in Studies 2 and 3); Check 3 passes in all three, through structure in Study 1.

**Check 1: antecedent signs within cells and within students.** For each input, estimate its association with the label within each structural cell (assessment group, deployment, course) and within each student, by rank correlation of within-cell or within-student ranks, with an interval clustered by student. Ranks are scaled to [0, 1] within each cell or student, so every group is centred at 0.5; students with a single request are dropped, and students whose requests share one label contribute nothing to the covariance. A conditional logistic regression by student, which uses only students with both labels, serves as a model-based check of the same sign. A rule whose sign holds in pooled data but not within cells encodes structure; a rule whose sign holds across students but not within them describes students rather than requests. In Studies 2 and 3 the student is the only cell below the cohort: every student belongs to one deployment or course, so lines 3 and 4 of Algorithm 1 coincide there.

**Check 2: trivial baselines.** Fit the same protocol with models that use no behaviour: one that sees only structural variables, one that predicts the label of a request from the label of the previous request in the same session (persistence), and one that uses the student's earlier share of positive labels (propensity). A variable is structural if its value is fixed by the course design, the record keeping or the clock independently of how the student works (course, week, session, position in session, elapsed time); it is behavioural if it results from the student's own actions. Table 1 applies this criterion. A behavioural rule model is informative to the extent that it improves on all three baselines, and the improvement is the quantity to report. The baselines are only a lower bound on what non-behavioural information can explain.

## 3.3 TSK rule bases
All rule bases are zero-order TSK systems [4]. Each input $x$ has two linguistic terms with ramp memberships $μ^H(x) = min{1, max{0, (x − q)/(Q − q)}}$ and $μ^L(x) = 1 − μ^H(x)$, where $q$ and $Q$ are the 25th and 75th percentiles of the training data. When $q = Q$ (for example, when most training values are zero) $Q$ is set to $q + 10^{−3}$, which makes the membership a step at $q$; Section 8 lists where this happens. With $k = 3$ inputs the full grid gives eight rules combined by the product t-norm, and the output is

!MATH figs/eq1.png

where $z_j ∈ [0, 1]$ is the consequent of rule $j$, $t_{ij}$ its term on input $i$, and the background term $w_0 = 0.05$, $z_0 = 0.5$ keeps $s$ defined when no rule fires. The learned rule bases fit all consequents jointly. Because $s$ is linear in $z$ once the firing degrees are known, $z$ solves the bounded least-squares problem

!MATH figs/eq2.png

with $π$ the training base rate and $α = 1$. With $α = 1$ and several hundred training cases the prior term is weak: a consequent is shrunk toward $π$ only for rules that almost no case fires, and a rule with little support at firing degree ≥ 0.5 can still receive an extreme consequent from cases that fire it partially. Section 8 reports a version with $α = N/10$ and marks weakly supported rules in the rule tables. In Study 1, CF-TSK uses log counts of three dialogue acts as inputs; the author-specified rule base FR1–FR5 uses four dialogue-act shares with consequents set by the author (Supplementary S1).

**SC-TSK** (Studies 2 and 3, Algorithm 2) ranks candidate behavioural inputs by $|$AUC − 0.5$|$ on the training data and admits an input only if its pooled direction is the same in two random halves of the training students. This is a stability filter, not Check 1: it does not condition on structure or on the student, and an input that proxies a structural cell or a student's propensity passes it. Check 1 is applied after construction. A variant that conditions on the student inside construction (SCw-TSK, which requires the within-student rank correlation to have the pooled sign in both halves) is reported in Section 8. **SM-TSK** adds the constraint $d_i (z_r − z_j) ≥ 0$ for every pair of rules $j$, $r$ that differ only in input $i$, with $d_i ∈ {−1, +1}$ the direction selected for that input (Algorithm 3). The constraints are linear, so the problem remains a small quadratic programme (eight variables, twelve constraints).

!MATH figs/prop1.png

Conditions of this kind are known for monotone TSK systems [31,33], and SM-TSK is an instance of constrained identification in the sense of [33]. We state and prove the version for our grid because it shows that SM-TSK guarantees a monotone output, not only monotone consequent pairs, and that the background term only shrinks the output toward $z_0$ by the factor $w_0/(1 + w_0) ≈ 0.048$ without affecting monotonicity. SC-TSK carries no such guarantee, and Section 8 measures how often its output moves against the selected directions on test data.

!FIG figs/alg2.png | Algorithm 2. SC-TSK. Line 5 is a stability filter on the pooled direction; Check 1 is applied after construction (Section 3.1).

!FIG figs/alg3.png | Algorithm 3. SM-TSK. Line 6 is solved by SLSQP from the SC-TSK solution (at most 500 iterations, tolerance 10⁻¹⁰); line 7, applied to the SC-TSK consequents, gives the sign violations in Table 7.

Algorithm 2 costs O(m N log N) for the AUCs and a bounded least-squares problem in eight variables; Algorithm 3 adds twelve linear constraints to the same problem. On synthetic data of the size used here (600 requests, nine candidates) Algorithm 2 took at most 0.07 s and Algorithm 3 at most 0.05 s over 20 repetitions.

Intervals are 95% student-cluster bootstrap intervals (2,000 resamples), and the verdicts of Algorithm 1 use them; where a plan named a Bonferroni-corrected level (Section 3.2) we also report that interval. Settings of all comparators are listed in Appendix A.

# 5. Study 2: answer-seeking queries on TutorTrace
## 5.1 Data, target and protocol
TutorTrace [15] records millisecond IDE telemetry from introductory Python sessions with an integrated AI tutor across four classroom deployments. Each AI query is labelled guided (asks for a hint or explanation) or dependent (asks for the answer or the code). The labels come from GPT-4o; the dataset authors report agreement with two human raters for deployments 1 and 2 of κ = .709 and .690 [15], which is substantial in the terms of Landis and Koch [29]. We use the released query-level metrics, which summarise activity since the previous query (code edits, characters inserted and deleted, terminal runs and errors, failed tests, idle and reading times). Chat text and code are not used. Deployment 1 (D1) contributes {{S.q.S2_D1_to_D2.n_train}} labelled queries ({{S.q.S2_D1_to_D2.pos_train}} dependent) from {{S.tt_counts['1'].students}} students, D2 {{S.q.S2_D1_to_D2.n_test}} ({{S.q.S2_D1_to_D2.pos_test}} dependent) from {{S.tt_counts['2'].students}}, and D3, a different task with unvalidated labels, {{S.q.S2_D12_to_D3.n_test}} ({{S.q.S2_D12_to_D3.pos_test}} dependent) from {{S.tt_counts['3'].students}}.

The plan fixed three decision rules for D1 → D2 at Bonferroni α = 0.0167: transfer (H1, lower bound of AUC above 0.5), behaviour beyond structure (H2, lower bound of the AUC gain of structure + SC-TSK over structure alone above 0), and non-inferiority to a logistic regression on all behavioural features (H3, margin 0.05). The planned structure model uses the query's position in the session, whether it is the first query, its time since session start and the test progress at the moment of asking. Test progress depends on the student's work and is behavioural by the criterion of Section 3.1, so H2 is also reported with structure restricted to the other three variables. The length of the query text was excluded because the label is assigned from that text.

## 5.2 Results

# 6. Study 3: production requests in EFL writing (RECIPE4U)
## 6.1 Data, target and protocol
RECIPE4U [16] contains student–ChatGPT exchanges from three EFL writing courses at one university, collected over a semester as students revised essays with ChatGPT. Every student utterance carries an intent label. We obtained the data through the authors' consent form, use utterance and essay texts only through their lengths, and redistribute nothing. Among request utterances we set y = 1 for production requests, which ask ChatGPT to generate, revise or translate the student's text, and y = 0 for guidance requests, which ask for information, evaluation, language-use advice or confirmation. The Scientific Writing course (SW) contributes {{S.rc_fmt.SW.n}} requests ({{S.q.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.pos_train}} production) from {{S.rc_fmt.SW.s}} students; Academic Writing (AW) and Intermediate Reading and Writing (IRW) together contribute {{S.q.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.n_test}} ({{S.q.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.pos_test}} production) from {{S.rc_fmt.AW.s}} and {{S.rc_fmt.IRW.s}} students.

The plan copies Study 2 with features computed from earlier exchanges of the same session: number of earlier turns, share of earlier requests that were production requests, earlier essay edits, mean earlier satisfaction rating, mean lengths of earlier student utterances and ChatGPT replies, and essay length and growth before the request. Structure is course, week, session and position in the session. Transfer is tested across courses: train on SW and test on AW and IRW together (primary), and the reverse. Unlike the text length excluded in Study 2, the share of earlier production requests uses labels of earlier requests only, which a system would have observed by the time of the next request. In deployment those labels would come from an intent classifier, whose errors would propagate; Section 6.2 simulates this. The propensity baseline uses the student's earlier sessions in the same course rather than earlier requests of the same session, because the persistence baseline already covers the session.

# 7. Validation on simulated data
The three studies show what the audit says on real data, but not whether its verdicts are right, because the true process is unknown. We therefore simulated interaction data with known generating processes, first with one cause per scenario (plan R3-G) and then with mixtures (plan R4-E). Each cohort has 100 students with one session each, assigned at random to three structural cells with logit offsets −1, 0 and 1; candidate inputs x2 and x3 are pure noise. Scenario A is structural: the label depends on the cell, and x1 is the cell offset plus noise. Scenario B is propensity: the label depends on a student intercept $a_s$ ~ N(0, 1), and $x_1 = a_s$ + noise. Scenario C is persistence: the label depends on the previous label in the session, and x1 is the share of earlier positives plus noise. Scenario D has a true within-student effect: logit p = offset + $a_s$ + e and $x_1$ = offset + $a_s$ + e, with e ~ N(0, 1) per request. Each replication generates a training and a test cohort, builds SC-TSK on the training cohort from {x1, x2, x3}, and records two verdicts about x1. The naive verdict calls x1 behavioural if it is selected and the held-out AUC is above chance. The audit verdict applies line 13 of Algorithm 1: Check 1 within cells and within students, and Check 2 against structure, persistence and propensity.

Three conclusions follow for the real data. Study 1 resembles scenario A without an effect, the case the audit rejects and naive transfer accepts. # 9. Discussion
The three studies answer the question in the title differently, and by the verdict rule of Algorithm 1 none of their rule bases is behavioural. In StudyChat the target was a downstream outcome tied to assessment structure, and every rule model, author-specified or learned, fuzzy or not, found the structure; Check 1 fails for the author-specified rule and Check 2 fails against structure. A planned cross-semester test did not protect against this, because the structure itself transfers: each semester has the same three assessment groups with similar relative difficulty. In TutorTrace and RECIPE4U the target was the request, and the same rule base transferred in all five splits, but the checks separate the two cases. In programming, Check 1 passes: the inputs keep their sign within students under two estimators. Check 2 fails, because the rule base does not beat structure, persistence and propensity together in any deployment, and whether it beats structure depends on what is counted as structure. In writing, structure explains nothing, but a student's own previous request explains nearly everything the rule base predicts, and only the lagged-label input passes Check 1. Reporting the checks separately is what makes these differences visible; a single pass/fail label would put the three studies in the same box. The simulation (Section 7) shows that the same rule rarely accepts a shortcut and detects within-student effects under structural and propensity confounds when they are large enough, but that it is conservative when persistence dominates.

Should fuzzy rule models be used for this task? On the same inputs they lose almost nothing against logistic regression, trees, EBM and monotone gradient boosting, so readability is cheap; the accuracy cost comes from using three inputs. Their advantages are a short rule table in linguistic terms and, with SM-TSK, a guarantee that the output moves in the stated direction of each input, which a monotone regression or monotone boosting also give but a tree or ANFIS does not. Their consequents are relative scores, not calibrated probabilities after transfer (Section 8). Their specific risk is that the rule text presents a shortcut as a behavioural sentence: a regression on assessment-group indicators announces what it uses, while an eight-rule table about writing requests does not, even when its predictions are driven by group structure. Consequents estimated rule by rule add a second risk, internal inconsistency, which in Study 2 affects well-supported cells (Section 5.2). We therefore recommend fuzzy rule models for help-seeking classification only together with the audit, with each check reported, SM-TSK tables shown instead of unconstrained ones, weakly supported rules marked, and the three baselines in the results table. Without the audit, the readability that makes them attractive is what makes their errors persuasive.

Three practical lessons follow. An antecedent's sign should be checked within the cells of whatever structure the data carry and within students, on the training data, before the rule is trusted or imposed as a constraint; in Study 1 that check would have shown at once that FR1 had no within-group support. Trivial baselines belong in every results table; they changed the conclusion of Study 1 and the interpretation of Studies 2 and 3. And what counts as structure is a modelling choice that should be stated and varied; in Study 2 it decides whether the rule base beats structure on D2.

Several alternative explanations bound the substantive reading. In StudyChat, students with few logged interactions may have used other AI tools, so low logged volume need not mean low use, and prior ability correlates with both exam scores and interaction style. In TutorTrace, the guided/dependent label is model-generated, and a dependent request may be a reasonable choice for a student who is stuck. In RECIPE4U, persistence predicts when the next production request comes but says nothing about why a student delegates, and a production request may be followed by careful revision. We did not evaluate the rule tables with instructors, so statements about their usefulness are hypotheses: whether instructors find them plausible, actionable or faithful to the model's behaviour remains to be tested.

# 10. Limitations
- Study 1 uses one course at one university; the structural confound is specific to its assessment design, though the check that found it is not. Its rules FR1–FR5 were written by the author from the source study rather than elicited from instructors.
- All analyses are exploratory. Only the plans of Study 3 and R2–R4 can be shown to have been public before their runs, several plans were written after earlier results on the same corpus, and Study 3's plan was written after Study 2's results were known.
- Studies 2 and 3 use one institution each. Study 2's sessions are 10–15 minutes long and its labels are model-generated; those of D3 were not validated. Study 3's grouping of intents into production and guidance requests is ours.
- The trivial baselines are a lower bound on what non-behavioural information can explain; a richer structural model could absorb more of the behavioural signal. The boundary between structure and behaviour is a modelling choice, which we state (Table 1) and vary only for test progress and the number of earlier turns.
- Within-student checks rest on few requests per student. In Study 3, SW has {{S.rc_fmt.SW.s}} students, of whom {{S.r3.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.fe.prev_production_share.counts.students_ge2}} made two or more requests and {{S.r3.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.fe.prev_production_share.counts.students_both_classes}} made requests of both kinds; only those {{S.r3.S3_SW_to_AW_IRW.fe.prev_production_share.counts.students_both_classes}} inform the within-student estimates. The simulation shows that power is low for small effects in short sessions.
- The simulation uses one structural, one propensity and one persistence mechanism at fixed strength, and a linear within-student effect; it does not cover every confound. Under persistence the audit has little power, so Study 3's failure of Check 2 cannot rule out a behavioural effect.
- TutorTrace's licence is stated as CC BY 4.0 in its metadata and marked as pending confirmation in its datasheet; we use only released numeric metrics and labels, redistribute nothing, and have asked the maintainers to confirm.
- None of the studies measures learning. Linking these patterns to learning requires data with unaided outcomes and comparison conditions, such as a randomised classroom study [17].
- The rule tables were not evaluated with users.

# 11. Conclusion
Fuzzy rule bases make models of student–AI interaction readable, and readability makes them persuasive. A rule base can nonetheless learn the structure of a course, the propensity of a student or the persistence of a label while its text describes behaviour. We proposed an audit that reports three checks separately, added monotone consequents with a guarantee on the output and measures that show where a rule table would mislead, and tested the audit on simulated data with known answers. On StudyChat the audit showed that a rule base which passed a planned transfer test had learned assessment structure. On TutorTrace it showed that the inputs of a rule base for answer-seeking queries keep their sign within students, but that the rule base does not beat every trivial baseline. On RECIPE4U it reduced an eight-rule base to a readable persistence rule. No rule base passed all three checks. We suggest that fuzzy models of learning data report these checks as routinely as they report accuracy.

# Appendix A. Settings of all models
All logistic regressions: inputs standardised, L2 penalty with C = 1, lbfgs solver, max_iter = 2000, no tuning (an unpenalised fit, C = 10⁶, is used only for the prior-ability adjustment of Supplementary S1). Conditional logistic regression: statsmodels 0.15, one standardised input at a time, Wald intervals. Decision trees: CART with depth 2 (and 3 in Study 1), min_samples_leaf = 10, random_state = 0. Wang–Mendel: the same LOW/HIGH ramps as the TSK models; each training case generates the rule of its maximal firing degree; a rule's consequent is the firing-weighted mean label of the cases that generated it; unoccupied rules are dropped. ANFIS-style TSK: eight rules, sigmoid memberships initialised at the quartile ramps (centre (q + Q)/2, slope 4/(Q − q)), consequents through a sigmoid initialised at the training base rate, memberships and consequents trained jointly by Adam (learning rate 0.01, 300 full-batch epochs, binary cross-entropy), predictions averaged over seeds 0–9. EBM: interpret 0.7.8, ExplainableBoostingClassifier with default settings and random_state = 0. No hyperparameter was tuned on held-out data. When fewer than three candidate inputs passed the sign check in an R2 refit, the best-ranked remaining candidate was added; the three cases are listed in results/r2_stats.json.
