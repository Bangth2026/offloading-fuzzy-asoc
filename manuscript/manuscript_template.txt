## Highlights
- Expert fuzzy rules for offloading risk rank students at chance on StudyChat.
- The main expert rule, unchecked acceptance of generated code, has the wrong sign.
- Eight fuzzy rules learned on dialogue-act volume transfer across semesters.
- The learned rule base matches logistic regression and reads as a rule table.
- Fuzzy constraints add little to a Transformer; analysis plans were fixed first.

## Abstract
Students using large language model (LLM) assistants can hand the reasoning to the model and still submit correct work, a pattern called cognitive offloading. Its indicators are graded and imprecise, which suggests fuzzy rules; but should the rules come from experts or from data? We compare both on StudyChat, real student–ChatGPT dialogues from two semesters of a university AI course, with 464 units from 175 students labelled by the gap between AI-assisted assignment and unaided exam scores. A five-rule expert Takagi–Sugeno–Kang (TSK) rule base, injected into a Transformer as Łukasiewicz implication constraints, leaves AUC unchanged over ten paired seeds (Δ = {{S.imp_d}}, Holm-adjusted p = {{S.imp_p}}), and on its own ranks units at chance (AUC {{S.f_auc}}): unchecked acceptance of generated code, its main antecedent, is inversely associated with the label (Spearman ρ = {{S.e6_v_rho}}). We then pre-specified a cross-semester test of a count-based TSK rule base (CF-TSK) with eight grid rules whose consequents are learned on one semester only. Trained on Fall 2024 and scored once on Spring 2025 (disjoint students), CF-TSK reaches AUC {{S.tp_cf_tsk}} (98.75% CI {{S.tp_h2.lo}} to {{S.tp_h2.hi}}), non-inferior to a logistic regression on all eight dialogue-act counts ({{S.tp_lr8}}) and statistically indistinguishable from a linear model on the same inputs ({{S.tp_lr3}}). The reverse direction gives {{S.tr_cf_tsk}}, and both directions hold when the inputs are selected inside the training semester. The learned rules agree across directions: low writing-request volume combined with pasted context marks risk. Fuzzy constraints add at most {{S.tr_h3.d}} AUC to the Transformer.

**Keywords:** Cognitive offloading; Takagi–Sugeno–Kang fuzzy rules; Expert knowledge; Learned consequents; Neuro-fuzzy learning; Cross-cohort validation; Explainable artificial intelligence; Learning analytics

# 1. Introduction
Interactive learning tools built on large language models give students on-demand access to explanations, code and worked solutions. A student can use that access to resolve a specific misconception, or to hand over the reasoning step itself while still submitting correct work. Course grades cannot separate the two cases, because both produce a passing submission. The cognitive-science literature calls the second pattern cognitive offloading, the use of external tools to reduce internal cognitive demand [1]; in a course, sustained offloading of reasoning, rather than of retrieval or arithmetic, risks leaving the target competency undeveloped while short-term performance holds.

Detecting offloading automatically meets two obstacles. Most public student–AI logs record usage volume or coarse task labels, not the turn-level detail needed to tell a student who asks a clarifying conceptual question from one who pastes an error message and asks for a corrected file. And no dataset labels offloading directly: it is a latent cognitive state, so any supervised model rests on a proxy. StudyChat [2] removes the first obstacle. It contains real, consented student–ChatGPT dialogues from a university AI course, annotated with a two-level dialogue-act schema and, since its latest release, linked to assignment and exam scores. Its originating study also motivates a proxy for the second obstacle: a cluster of students whose turns were dominated by writing-delegation requests averaged 83.8% on exams against 89.0% for a cluster dominated by targeted coding questions, although both scored above 93% on the AI-assisted assignments [2]. A gap between assisted and unaided performance is what offloading theory predicts.

A third difficulty is specific to the behavioural evidence and is the reason this paper takes a soft-computing approach. The quantities an instructor would reason with (the share of generated outputs accepted without a following edit, the length of runs of assignment-specific questions, the balance between pasting context and verifying answers) are rates, not events. A student who accepts 48% of generated outputs unchecked is not categorically different from one who accepts 52%, yet a threshold rule treats them as opposite cases. Our earlier version of this system used such crisp rules for its explanations and a linear average of the rates as a training target, and found that pulling the network toward that average left accuracy unchanged. Fuzzy sets [3] give a principled alternative: each rate is described by overlapping linguistic terms (LOW, HIGH), each rule fires to a degree, and a TSK rule base [4] turns those degrees into a graded rule score. Differentiable fuzzy logic also offers a different way to inject rules into a network: instead of regressing every prediction toward one target, a rule can be read as a fuzzy implication that is violated only when its antecedent holds and the prediction disagrees with its consequent [5–7].

Fuzzy rules raise a prior question that is easy to skip: where do the rules come from? Expert rules encode what instructors believe offloading looks like, and they are readable by construction. Rules whose consequents are learned from data, in the tradition of ANFIS [22], can correct a wrong belief but can also overfit a small cohort. A course that adopts either kind wants to know whether the rules hold for the next cohort of students, not only within cross-validation on the current one. StudyChat makes that question testable, because its two semesters are separate offerings of the same course with disjoint students.

This paper makes three contributions.

- We formulate cognitive-offloading detection as a weakly supervised problem over student–LLM dialogue turns, with a label built from the assessment structure documented in the dataset release (464 units, 175 students), and test an expert five-rule TSK rule base both as a standalone score and as a Łukasiewicz implication constraint on a Transformer encoder, under decision rules fixed before the experiments. The expert rules fail, and we show which antecedent fails and in which direction.
- We propose a count-based TSK rule base (CF-TSK) whose eight grid rules assume no direction and whose consequents are learned on training data only, together with an interval form of the implication loss that accepts any consequent in [0, 1].
- We evaluate CF-TSK in a cross-semester design specified in writing before it was run: train on one semester, score the other once. The learned rule base transfers in both directions, matches the strongest aggregate baseline, and yields a rule table whose pattern is stable across semesters. We report alongside it the comparisons that did not succeed.

Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 states the problem, Section 4 describes the data and Section 5 the method. Sections 6 and 7 give the protocol and results, and Sections 8 to 11 discuss findings, limitations, ethics and conclusions.

# 2. Related work
## 2.1 Cognitive offloading and over-reliance on AI
Cognitive offloading was introduced to describe the use of physical action or external tools to lower the information-processing demand of a task [1]. In education the construct has recently been extended to LLM-assisted work along several lines. Bastani et al. [8] show experimentally that unrestricted generative-AI access can reduce learning gains even while assisted-phase performance improves. Fan et al. [9] describe metacognitive laziness, the suppression of self-monitoring when fluent answers are easy to obtain, and Kosmyna et al. [10] report reduced EEG connectivity and weaker essay recall among writers who used an LLM. Lee et al. [11] find that knowledge workers' self-reported reductions in cognitive effort track their confidence in the AI rather than task difficulty, and Köbis et al. [12] show that delegating a decision to AI can increase dishonest behaviour. On the measurement side, Zheng et al. [13] describe reliance patterns in field-collected student–ChatGPT conversations, Hou et al. [14] validate a self-report reliance scale, and RelianceScope [15] codes chat and code-edit logs into nine qualitative reliance patterns. These studies are experimental, self-report or qualitative. The present work instead builds a predictive, turn-level risk score supervised by an objective performance-gap proxy and evaluates it quantitatively.

## 2.2 Sequential models of students
Knowledge tracing estimates evolving mastery from sequences of graded attempts, from Bayesian knowledge tracing to Deep Knowledge Tracing [16], self-attentive knowledge tracing [17] and the context-aware attentive model AKT [18]. These architectures assume a correctness signal at every step. Offloading detection has none; here the sequential unit is the behavioural category of a dialogue turn, and supervision comes from an assessment-level outcome, which requires the group-aware aggregation of Sections 3 and 5.

## 2.3 Dialogue-act analysis of student–LLM interaction
The StudyChat study annotates 16,851 student utterances with 8 broad and 31 specific dialogue acts and reports that utterance count adds little to a prior-performance baseline (mean R² gain of 0.006 to 0.018), whereas specific dialogue-act composition explains substantially more (mean R² of 0.40 to 0.49) [2]. This motivates modelling the category and order of turns rather than usage volume, and avoiding usage frequency as a risk indicator in itself.

## 2.4 Injecting rules into neural networks: neuro-symbolic and neuro-fuzzy routes
Neuro-symbolic AI combines the transparency of expert rules with the predictive capacity of neural networks [19,20]. One family of methods injects first-order rules into training through soft constraints: Hu et al. [5] distil logic rules into a network through a teacher built from rule-regularised posteriors, the semantic loss of Xu et al. [6] penalises probability mass on assignments that violate a propositional constraint, and Logic Tensor Networks [21] ground first-order formulas in fuzzy truth values that are maximised during learning. Van Krieken et al. [7] analyse which fuzzy operators behave well under gradient descent and show that the choice of implication shapes the gradients a rule sends to the network. A second family is the classical neuro-fuzzy line, in which a fuzzy rule base is itself the model: ANFIS [22] tunes the membership functions and consequents of a TSK system [4] by gradient learning, building on the linguistic control tradition of Mamdani and Assilian [23]. Our design sits between the two. The rule base is not the predictor and is not trained; it stays as expert-authored knowledge with data-fitted breakpoints, and it constrains a separate sequence model through an implication loss. In education, Hooshyar et al. [24] argue that deep learner models need injected educational knowledge to be deployable responsibly and add curriculum and prerequisite knowledge to a knowledge-tracing model. We share the aim but differ in construct (behaviour toward an LLM rather than prerequisite structure) and in mechanism (graded fuzzy implications rather than crisp constraints). Within the 24 works reviewed here, we found none that applies fuzzy rule constraints to the detection of cognitive offloading in student–LLM dialogue.

# 3. Problem statement and formulation
Given the turn-level dialogue between a student and an LLM assistant while the student completes a group of related assignments, and the student's later score on the proctored exam covering the same material, the task is to estimate a continuous offloading-risk score that (a) correlates with the gap between assisted and unaided performance, (b) is computable from behaviour observed before the exam, and (c) comes with a justification an instructor can audit. No direct label for offloading exists, so the target is built from an observable proxy, and the model's claims are bounded by how well that proxy tracks the construct (Section 9).

Let student i complete assignment a with a sequence of turns T = (t_1, …, t_n). Each turn t_k has a feature vector x_k with (1) the specific dialogue-act label; (2) a verbatim-acceptance indicator, set when a Writing turn is not followed in the same conversation by an Editing or Verification turn; (3) inter-turn latency; (4) a self-attempt indicator, set when a Writing request is preceded in the same conversation by a Provide Context turn carrying pasted code or error output; and (5) a surface-dependency indicator, set inside a run of three or more consecutive Contextual Questions turns not interrupted by a Conceptual Questions turn.

The unit of analysis follows the course's assessment structure, which the dataset documents directly: assignments a1 and a2 precede Exam 1, a3 and a4 precede Exam 2, and a5 to a7 precede the Final Exam. A unit is therefore a (student, semester, assessment-group) triple, up to three per student per semester. For group g with assignments {a} and following exam e, let llm_g be the assignment score weighted by each assignment's documented maximum points and no_llm_g the score on exam e. The weak target is y = clip((llm_g − no_llm_g) / llm_g, 0, 1), so that students who do better unaided than assisted, the expected pattern for productive use, receive zero risk. The target can also be depressed by exam difficulty, by topic misalignment between a group and its exam, and by ordinary variance; Section 9 discusses these threats. Models are evaluated by the correlation of r with y on held-out students and by classifying the top quartile of y, with folds constrained so that all units of a student stay together (Section 6.1).

!CODE
Algorithm 1. Assessment-group weak-label construction
Input : score table S (a1..a7, e1..e3 per student and semester);
        max-points table MP[semester][assessment]  (scores/README.md)
Output: label set L = {(student, semester, group, y)}
1  G <- {(g1,[a1,a2],e1), (g2,[a3,a4],e2), (g3,[a5,a6,a7],e3)}
2  L <- empty list
3  for each row s in S:
4      for each (name, assigns, exam) in G:
5          llm_g    <- sum(s[a]*MP[s.sem][a] for a in assigns) / sum(MP[s.sem][a] for a in assigns)
6          no_llm_g <- s[exam]
7          y        <- clip((llm_g - no_llm_g) / llm_g, 0, 1)
8          append (s.userId, s.sem, name, y) to L
9  return L
!END

Algorithm 1 is deterministic, uses only quantities documented in the dataset release, and has no free parameter beyond the clipping range.

# 4. Dataset
StudyChat contains 2,214 student–ChatGPT dialogues from 203 consenting students in an undergraduate AI course (COMPSCI 383) at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, Fall 2024 and Spring 2025, with 16,851 utterances across seven graded programming assignments [2]. Each utterance carries a dialogue act from the schema in Table 1, assigned by prompting GPT-4.1. Two agreement figures must be kept apart. During schema development two annotators reached Cohen's κ of 0.910 (broad) and 0.788 (specific) on a curated set. On a separate held-out set of 154 utterances, agreement between GPT-4.1 and humans was 0.582 (broad) and 0.493 (specific), on par with human–human agreement on the same set (0.578 and 0.538). The held-out figure is the relevant one for features derived at scale, and Section 9 returns to it. Release v1.1 (Spring 2026) added assignment and exam scores for consenting students, which the target in Section 3 requires.

The dataset is licensed CC BY 4.0 and access-gated on Hugging Face (wmcnicho/StudyChat). We downloaded dialogues/f24_labeled_dialogues.jsonl (232,987,304 bytes) and dialogues/s25_labeled_dialogues.jsonl (390,499,753 bytes) through the huggingface_hub API on 22 August 2026; both sizes matched the repository listing and the combined record count (6,864 + 9,987 = 16,851) matched the published total. Personally identifiable information was removed by the original authors through an iterative regular-expression pipeline (6,413 likely instances removed, none found in a second pass); the data are still treated as real student data under the licence. Matching against the scores release (Section 7.1) yields the 175-student analysis sample.

# 5. Proposed method
## 5.1 Pipeline overview
Figure 1 shows the pipeline. Score tables and dialogue turns are processed separately by Algorithm 1 (weak labels) and Algorithm 2 (turn features and unit-level rates), then merged into the unit dataset that every model in Table 3 consumes. The fuzzy rule base of Section 5.3 takes the four unit-level rates as input. It is used twice: as a constraint inside the Transformer's training loss (Section 5.4), and as the source of the explanation attached to each score.

!FIG figs/fig1_pipeline.png | Figure 1. Pipeline from the two StudyChat releases to the risk score and its explanation. Algorithm 1 builds weak labels, Algorithm 2 extracts turn features and the unit rates v, u, p, e, and Algorithm 3 trains the Transformer with the fuzzy implication loss. The fuzzy rule base is fitted on each training fold, injected into the loss (solid purple arrow) and used to explain each score (dashed arrow).

!CODE
Algorithm 2. Turn-level feature extraction and unit rates
Input : turns D = (d_1..d_m) of one (student, semester), fields
        (chatId, interactionCount, timestamp, llm_label)
Output: feature sequence X = (x_1..x_m)
1  sort D by (chatId, interactionCount)
2  for k = 1..m:
3      (broad_k, specific_k) <- split(d_k.llm_label, '>')
4      latency_k  <- timestamp_k - timestamp_{k-1} if same chatId else 0
5      verbatim_k <- 1 if specific_k in WRITING and no later turn of the
                     same chatId is in EDIT_VERIFY, else 0
6      self_k     <- 1 if broad_k = WRITING and an earlier turn of the
                     same chatId has broad = PROVIDE_CONTEXT, else 0
7      surf_k     <- 1 if the current run of CONTEXTUAL_QUESTIONS turns
                     (reset by CONCEPTUAL_QUESTIONS) has length >= 3, else 0
8      x_k <- (specific_k, latency_k, verbatim_k, self_k, surf_k)
9  unit rates over the turns of an assessment group:
      v = mean verbatim over WRITING turns;  u = mean surf;
      p = share of PROVIDE_CONTEXT turns;    e = share of VERIFICATION turns
10 return X, (v, u, p, e)
!END

Category identifiers in Algorithm 2 are the lowercase, underscore-separated strings of the released llm_label field (Appendix A), not the display names of Table 1.

## 5.2 Sequential encoder
Each turn is embedded by concatenating a learned embedding of its specific dialogue act (the 31 schema categories plus one stray label, misc>error, that occurs in five turns of the release) with a linear projection of its scalar and binary features. The sequence is encoded by a Transformer encoder with 2 layers, 2 attention heads and model dimension 32, a size chosen for a corpus of a few hundred labelled sequences, with learned positional embeddings over turn index. A learned [ASSIGN] pooling token, analogous to the classification token of BERT-style encoders, attends over the sequence, and its final representation feeds a two-layer head with sigmoid output that produces the risk score r ∈ (0, 1).

## 5.3 Fuzzy behavioural rule base
**Inputs and membership functions.** The rule base reads four unit-level rates from Algorithm 2: v (verbatim acceptance), u (surface dependency), p (provide-context share) and e (verification share). Each input x has two linguistic terms with ramp membership functions,

!EQ μ_HIGH(x) = min(1, max(0, (x − a) / (b − a))),    μ_LOW(x) = 1 − μ_HIGH(x),    (1)

where a and b are the 25th and 75th percentiles of that input on the training fold. Fitting the breakpoints inside each fold keeps test units out of the rule base; using quantiles rather than hand-set values makes the terms relative to the cohort, so that HIGH means high for this course. Setting μ_HIGH to a step at (a + b)/2 gives the crisp control used in Sections 7.3 and 7.4 (Figure 2).

!FIG figs/fig_membership.png | Figure 2. Ramp membership functions for one input. The breakpoints a and b are the training-fold quartiles. The dashed step at their midpoint is the crisp control, which assigns units just either side of the midpoint to opposite terms.

**Rules.** Table 2 lists the five rules. FR1 to FR3 are the graded counterparts of the rules R1 to R3 of the crisp design; FR4 and FR5 encode the protective reading of verification and editing in Table 1 and close the rule base for students with no delegation pattern. The rules were fixed from R1 to R3 and Table 1 before any fuzzy experiment was run; in particular, no rule was derived from the ablation in Section 7.5, which used the full dataset and would leak information into the rule base.

!TABLE
Rule | Antecedent | Consequent z_j | Origin | Explanation text
FR1 | v is HIGH | 1.00 | R1 | Generated output is usually accepted without a following edit or check.
FR2 | u is HIGH | 0.75 | R2 | Long runs of assignment-specific questions without a conceptual question.
FR3 | p is HIGH and e is LOW | 1.00 | R3 | Code or errors are pasted for the model to fix, with little verification.
FR4 | v is LOW and e is HIGH | 0.00 | Table 1 | Output is checked or edited rather than accepted as-is.
FR5 | v is LOW and u is LOW and p is LOW | 0.00 | Table 1 | No delegation pattern is present.
!END
!CAP Table 2. The fuzzy behavioural rule base. FR2 has a lower consequent because the source study links contextual questioning to weaker outcomes less directly than delegation.

**Inference.** With the product t-norm, the firing degree of rule j for unit i is w_ij = Π_(x,T)∈A_j μ_T(x_i), where A_j is the rule's antecedent. A zero-order TSK system gives the rule score

!EQ s_i = (Σ_j w_ij z_j + w_0 z_0) / (Σ_j w_ij + w_0),    (2)

where a background term w_0 = 0.05, z_0 = 0.5 keeps s defined when no rule fires. The explanation for unit i lists the rules with the two highest firing degrees above 0.2, each with its degree and the text of Table 2; for example, "FR1 (0.86): generated output is usually accepted without a following edit or check".

## 5.4 Injecting the rules into training
The base objective is binary cross-entropy between r and the top-quartile label y_bin. A rule term is added with weight λ, L = BCE(r, y_bin) + λ L_rule. We compare three rule terms.

**Linear target (the earlier design).** L_lin = mean (r_i − s̃_i)², where s̃ is the min–max-normalised mean of v, u and max(0, p − e). This regresses every prediction toward a single average of the rates, whether or not any rule applies.

**TSK target.** L_tsk = mean (r_i − s_i)², with s from Eq. (2). The target is now produced by the rule base, but it still acts on every unit.

**Fuzzy implication (proposed).** Each rule is read as a fuzzy implication from its antecedent to a statement about r and is evaluated with the Łukasiewicz implication I(a, c) = min(1, 1 − a + c), whose violation 1 − I is max(0, a − c) [7]. For a risk-raising rule (z_j ≥ 0.5) the consequent is "r is at least z_j"; for a risk-lowering rule it is "r is low", with truth value 1 − r. This gives

!EQ L_imp = (1/|B|·|J|) Σ_i [ Σ_{j∈J+} max(0, w_ij z_j − r_i) + Σ_{j∈J−} max(0, r_i − (1 − w_ij)) ],    (3)

where J+ and J− are the raising and lowering rules. A unit on which no rule fires contributes nothing, and a unit on which a rule fires only weakly contributes in proportion to that degree. This is the property the linear target lacks, and it is the reason we expect the implication form to bind the model to the rules where they are informative without disturbing it elsewhere. Because the rule base is not updated by gradient, the rules stay exactly as authored, and the firing degrees used for the explanation are the same quantities that constrained training.

!CODE
Algorithm 3. Group-aware cross-validated training with fuzzy rule constraints
Input : units U = {(group_key, X, y, v, u, p, e)}; rule term K in
        {none, linear, tsk, implication}; weight lambda; folds k = 5; seed
Output: fold AUC, Pearson r, out-of-fold predictions and firing degrees
1  y_bin <- 1[y >= quantile_0.75(y)]
2  folds <- StratifiedGroupKFold(k, stratify = y_bin, groups = group_key, seed)
3  for (train, test) in folds:
4      skip fold if y_bin[train] has one class
5      FRB <- fit breakpoints (a, b) on rates of train only          // Eq. (1)
6      (s, W)[train] <- FRB.score(train)                              // Eq. (2)
7      train Transformer on train with loss BCE + lambda * L_K        // Sec. 5.4
8      r[test] <- predict(test);  (s, W)[test] <- FRB.score(test)
9      record AUC(y_bin[test], r[test]), pearson(r[test], y[test]), violation L_imp(test)
10 return means over folds, out-of-fold r, s, W
!END

Grouping by student and semester in line 2 keeps a student's units in one fold; a plain stratified split at line 2 would leak information and was not used anywhere.

## 5.5 A count-based rule base with learned consequents (CF-TSK)
The expert rule base reads proportions of turns and fixes the direction of every rule. CF-TSK changes both choices. Its inputs are x_W, x_C and x_P, the logarithms of one plus the number of writing_request, conceptual_questions and provide_context turns in a unit, so they keep the volume information that shares discard. Each input has the same LOW and HIGH ramp memberships as in Section 5.3, with breakpoints at the training 25th and 75th percentiles. The rule base is the full grid of 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 rules, from "W LOW and C LOW and P LOW" to "W HIGH and C HIGH and P HIGH", combined by the product t-norm, so no rule asserts in advance whether a pattern raises or lowers risk. The zero-order TSK output is s = (Σ_j w_j z_j + w_0 z_0) / (Σ_j w_j + w_0), with w_0 = 0.05 and z_0 = 0.5 as before.

Because s is linear in the consequents once the firing degrees are known, the consequents z ∈ [0, 1]^8 are fitted on the training units in closed form, by bounded least squares against the binary label with a ridge term α‖z − π‖², where π is the training base rate and α = 1. A rule that fires rarely therefore stays near the base rate instead of taking an extreme value from a few units. The result is a table of eight numbers that an instructor can read directly: each is the estimated probability of high risk for units that fully match one combination of LOW and HIGH volumes.

To use a learned rule base as a constraint, the implication loss must accept any consequent, not only 0 or 1. We read rule j as "if the antecedent holds, risk is about z_j" and take the Łukasiewicz bounds w_j z_j ≤ r ≤ 1 − w_j (1 − z_j). The interval implication loss is the mean of relu(w_j z_j − r) + relu(r − 1 + w_j(1 − z_j)) over rules and units. When a rule does not fire (w_j = 0) both bounds are vacuous; when it fires fully, r is pulled toward z_j from whichever side it violates. For z_j = 1 and z_j = 0 the loss reduces to the raising and lowering terms of Section 5.4.

# 6. Experimental design
## 6.1 Data split
Splits use StratifiedGroupKFold with five folds, grouped on student identifier and semester and stratified on the top-quartile label, so that a student's Group-1 behaviour never informs a model tested on the same student's Group-2 or Group-3 unit.

## 6.2 Baselines
The logistic-regression baseline reproduces the aggregate dialogue-act-count regression reported for StudyChat [2], adapted to top-quartile classification. The DKT-style baseline keeps the LSTM architecture but removes dialogue-act identity, keeping only the scalar behavioural flags, to test whether a knowledge-tracing recurrence without behavioural categories suffices [16].

## 6.3 Metrics and pre-specified comparisons
Primary metrics are AUC for top-quartile classification and the Pearson correlation between r and y, both on held-out folds. For the rule terms we add two consistency metrics computed on held-out units: the Spearman correlation between r and the fuzzy rule score s, and the mean implication violation of Eq. (3). The following were fixed before the fuzzy experiments were run. E1: ten seeds, each giving paired 5-fold AUCs for λ = 0, linear, TSK and implication (λ = 1), compared by two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (implication, TSK and linear against λ = 0; implication against linear) with Holm correction over the four tests, paired t-tests and Cohen's d as secondary statistics. E2: λ ∈ {0, 0.3, 0.6, 1.0} for each rule term over three seeds. E3: out-of-fold AUC of the rule scores themselves (linear, crisp, fuzzy), with a student-level cluster bootstrap (2,000 resamples) for AUC differences. E4: sensitivity of the rule-score AUC to the breakpoint quantiles over a 3 × 4 grid, for fuzzy and crisp memberships. E5: firing profiles of the units flagged by the implication model. The reading of each result was also fixed in advance: an accuracy difference is called significant only if its Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon p is below 0.05; a difference between rule scores is called reliable only if its 95% bootstrap interval excludes zero; one membership scheme is called more stable than another only if its AUC standard deviation across the breakpoint grid is lower; a rule term is said to reduce rule violation only if held-out violation falls by at least 10% relative to λ = 0; and, should a rule term cost accuracy, the recommended weight is the smallest λ in the sweep whose AUC stays within one seed standard deviation of λ = 0. Sections 7.3 to 7.6 apply these rules as stated. Experiments E1–E6 were run once with these rules. The follow-up E7–E9 in Section 7.8 was designed after E6 and specified in writing before it was run; it is reported separately and does not enter the decisions above.

## 6.4 Cross-semester confirmation (E10)
The pooled cross-validation of Sections 6.1–6.3 mixes two cohorts in every fold. E10 asks the deployment question instead: does a model fitted on one offering of the course rank the students of the next one? Fall 2024 contributes {{S.tp_ntr}} units from 65 students and Spring 2025 {{S.tp_nte}} units from 110; no student appears in both. In the primary direction every fitted quantity (CF-TSK breakpoints and consequents, scalers, regression weights, network weights) is estimated on Fall 2024, and Spring 2025 is scored once. The high-risk threshold is the Fall 2024 75th percentile of y, applied unchanged to Spring 2025 (prevalence {{S.t_prev}}). The reverse direction, Spring 2025 to Fall 2024, is reported as a secondary analysis.

The design was written down and committed to the code repository (PREREG_E10.md) before the analysis was run. It names four hypotheses, tested at Bonferroni α = 0.0125 with 98.75% student-cluster bootstrap intervals (2,000 resamples):
- H1, non-inferiority: the lower bound of AUC(CF-TSK) − AUC(LR8) is above −0.05, where LR8 is the logistic regression on the eight broad counts and the margin of 0.05 was fixed in advance;
- H2, transfer: the lower bound of AUC(CF-TSK) is above 0.5;
- H3, constraint: a Transformer trained with the interval implication loss under CF-TSK beats the unconstrained Transformer over ten paired seeds (Wilcoxon p < 0.0125, mean difference > 0);
- H4, complementarity: the lower bound of AUC(rank fusion of LR8 and CF-TSK) − AUC(LR8) is above 0.

A logistic regression on the same three log counts (LR3) separates the effect of the fuzzy structure from the effect of the inputs. One threat is not removed by the split: the three input categories are those with the largest drops in the pooled ablation of Section 7.5, which used both semesters. E10 is therefore a confirmation on a split that no model was fitted or evaluated on, not a test on data that informed no choice. E11 (PREREG_E11.md) repeats E10 with the input selection moved inside the training semester, under the same CF-TSK construction, with H1 and H2 tested at Bonferroni α = 0.025.

## 7.3 The fuzzy rule score as a predictor in its own right
Before asking whether the rules help the network, we asked whether they carry signal at all. Computed out of fold, with breakpoints fitted on the training folds, the fuzzy rule score ranks units with AUC {{S.f_auc}} (Spearman with y {{S.f_rho}}), against {{S.c_auc}} for the crisp step version of the same rules and {{S.l_auc}} for the linear average used in the earlier design. The cluster-bootstrap difference between fuzzy and crisp scores is {{S.fc_d}} (95% CI {{S.fc_lo}} to {{S.fc_hi}}) and between fuzzy and linear {{S.fl_d}} (95% CI {{S.fl_lo}} to {{S.fl_hi}}). {% if S.fc_sig and S.fc_pos %}Grading the memberships therefore adds ranking signal beyond thresholding the same rules.{% elif S.fc_sig %}The crisp version ranks units better than the graded one, so on these data the fuzzy form does not earn its place through ranking power.{% else %}The fuzzy and crisp scores rank units similarly, so the case for the fuzzy form cannot rest on ranking power; it has to rest on stability (Section 7.4) and on how the rules enter training (Section 7.6).{% endif %} {% if S.fl_sig and S.fl_pos %}The rule base also ranks units better than the linear average of the same rates, which supports replacing that average as the rule signal.{% elif S.fl_sig %}The linear average of the rates ranks units better than the rule base, a point we return to in Section 8.{% else %}Against the linear average of the same rates the difference is not reliable.{% endif %} All three should be read against the logistic regression (AUC {{S.b_lr_broad}}): {% if S.f_near_chance %}the rule score is close to chance, well below what a regression fitted on dialogue-act counts achieves.{% elif S.f_auc_num < 0.65 %}five expert rules with no learned weights fall short of a regression fitted on dialogue-act counts, as expected.{% else %}five expert rules with no learned weights match or exceed a regression fitted on dialogue-act counts, which we did not expect and which deserves replication before it is relied on.{% endif %}

To see why, we checked the direction of each rule input against the label. No parameter is fitted in this check, so it needs no folds; confidence intervals come from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of students. Verbatim acceptance v, the antecedent of FR1 and of FR4, has Spearman ρ = {{S.e6_v_rho}} with y (95% CI {{S.e6_v_lo}} to {{S.e6_v_hi}}; AUC {{S.e6_v_auc}}). The per-turn share of unchecked writing requests, used in Section 7.7, behaves the same way (ρ = {{S.e6_va_rho}}, CI {{S.e6_va_lo}} to {{S.e6_va_hi}}). The other three inputs have small positive correlations whose intervals include zero: surface dependency ρ = {{S.e6_u_rho}} ({{S.e6_u_lo}} to {{S.e6_u_hi}}), provide-context share {{S.e6_p_rho}} ({{S.e6_p_lo}} to {{S.e6_p_hi}}) and verification share {{S.e6_e_rho}} ({{S.e6_e_lo}} to {{S.e6_e_hi}}). {% if S.e6_v_neg %}FR1 assumed that accepting generated output without a following edit or check marks offloading. On StudyChat, units with more unchecked acceptance show a smaller assisted–unaided gap, not a larger one. Because FR1 has the largest consequent and fires most often (mean v = {{S.e6_v_mean}}), a wrong sign on FR1 can offset the weak positive signal of FR2 and FR3, which is consistent with a rule score near 0.5. Two readings are possible and the data cannot separate them. Students who are already strong may accept correct code because it is correct, which makes unchecked acceptance a marker of competence. Alternatively, the "no later Editing or Verification turn" condition may miss checking that students do outside the chat, for example by running the code. In both cases the rule encodes an assumption the data contradict.{% endif %}

## 7.4 Sensitivity to membership breakpoints
Crisp rules depend on where the thresholds sit, and the fuzzy rules depend on their breakpoints. We varied the lower breakpoint quantile over {0.1, 0.2, 0.3} and the upper over {0.6, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9}, refitting the rule base inside each training fold. The fuzzy rule-score AUC ranges from {{S.e4f_min}} to {{S.e4f_max}} (SD {{S.e4f_sd}}) across the twelve settings, and the crisp score from {{S.e4c_min}} to {{S.e4c_max}} (SD {{S.e4c_sd}}) (Figure 5). {% if S.e4_stable %}The graded memberships are therefore less sensitive to where the breakpoints sit than the thresholds are, which is the behaviour fuzzy sets are meant to provide: a unit near a breakpoint changes its membership gradually instead of switching rule.{% if S.f_near_chance %} The point should not be overstated: a score that sits near chance at every setting is stable partly because it has little signal to lose. The result shows that graded memberships remove threshold artefacts, not that they make a weak rule base informative.{% endif %}{% else %}On this grid the graded memberships are not less sensitive to breakpoint placement than the thresholds, so we make no robustness claim for them.{% endif %} The grid is reported as a sensitivity analysis; the quartile setting used throughout was fixed in advance and was not selected from it.

Why constrain the network rather than filter its output afterwards? A post-hoc filter and a model can contradict each other unit by unit with nothing to reconcile them. Under the implication loss, the rules that justify a flag are the rules the model was trained to respect, and λ sets how strongly. That argument holds only if the rules are right, and Section 7.3 shows that FR1 is not right on these data; a constraint built from a wrong-signed rule can at best be ignored by the network, which is what the unchanged AUC and violation suggest happened here. These are interpretability arguments; if accuracy alone mattered, the aggregate logistic regression would be the simplest defensible choice on these data.

## 7.8 Follow-up: outcome-checked rules with learned consequents
The direction check raised an obvious question: would the rules help once they agree with the data? Section 7.3 had already been run on the same units, so any answer is informed by it. We therefore wrote the follow-up design down and committed it to the code repository (PREREG_E7_E9.md) before running it, fixed every setting in advance, and ran each variant once. The outcome-checked rule base (OC-TSK) keeps FR1–FR5 and the quartile memberships. It adds two rules taken from the source study [2]: FR6 (writing-request share HIGH and conceptual-question share LOW → risk) and FR7 (conceptual-question share HIGH → no risk). Our own ablation (Section 7.5) points to the same two categories, so this choice is not independent of the data. Consequents are learned on each training fold by bounded least squares with a ridge pull (α = 1) toward the expert values. The network is constrained by an interval form of the implication loss, wⱼzⱼ ≤ r ≤ 1 − wⱼ(1 − zⱼ), which reduces to the E1 terms when zⱼ is 0 or 1. Four paired comparisons over the same ten seeds were Holm-adjusted together. The aggregate logistic regression was refitted inside the same folds and reaches {{S.fu_lr_m}} ± {{S.fu_lr_sd}}, in line with its fifteen-seed estimate in Table 3 ({{S.b_lrs.mean}} ± {{S.b_lrs.sd}}). # 8. Discussion
The two halves of this study answer the question in the title differently. Expert rules failed in every test we ran: as a standalone score, as a constraint on the Transformer, and when transferred between semesters. Rules with the same fuzzy machinery but learned consequents and volume inputs transferred in both directions, matched the strongest aggregate baseline, and produced a rule table whose pattern held across cohorts. The difference between the two lies in what the rules know, not in how they are computed, and it is the main result of the paper. For applied soft computing it means that the value of a fuzzy rule base in a new domain depends first on whether its knowledge can be checked against outcomes, and second, when it fails that check, on whether the consequents can be learned from training data. Readability did not have to be traded for accuracy: CF-TSK is statistically indistinguishable in accuracy from the linear model on the same inputs, and its explanation is eight numbers attached to rules in plain language.

The direction of the learned rules is the substantive finding for learning analytics. Under the expert view, heavy use of writing requests is the signature of offloading. In StudyChat the larger assisted–unaided gap belongs to students who paste code and error messages but rarely ask the assistant to write, and to students who use it little overall. Several mechanisms could produce this. Students who struggle may use the assistant to repair code rather than to produce it. Heavy users may learn more from the generated code than the offloading view assumes. The exam may also reward exactly what frequent interaction practises. The data cannot separate these, and the label is a proxy (Section 9). What the data do show is that a rule an instructor would write first is contradicted by outcomes in two independent cohorts.

The design choices follow from published evidence: the weak target uses the dataset's own assessment structure and scoring formula, and modelling dialogue-act sequences rather than usage counts follows from the source study's regression comparison [2]. Because the target is a proxy, a high risk score indicates a behavioural pattern statistically associated with a larger assisted–unaided gap in this population, not a diagnosis of a student's cognitive state.

The main methodological question is what fuzzy rules add over the crisp and linear forms that preceded them. Three answers are possible in principle, and the experiments were set up to separate them. The fuzzy score may rank students better than its crisp counterpart (Section 7.3); it may be less sensitive to arbitrary breakpoint choices (Section 7.4); and the implication form may constrain the network where rules fire without the side effects of regressing every unit toward one target (Section 7.6). On ranking, {% if S.fc_sig and S.fc_pos %}the fuzzy score is reliably better than its crisp counterpart{% elif S.fc_sig %}the crisp counterpart is reliably better, so this answer is not supported{% else %}the two are indistinguishable, so this answer is not supported{% endif %}. On stability, {% if S.e4_stable %}the fuzzy score varies less across breakpoints than the crisp score does{% else %}the fuzzy score is not more stable than the crisp one, so this answer is not supported either{% endif %}. On training, {% if S.imp_sig and S.imp_pos %}the implication loss improves AUC significantly{% elif S.imp_sig %}the implication loss reduces AUC significantly{% else %}the implication loss leaves AUC unchanged{% endif %} and {% if S.viol_drop %}reduces rule violation on held-out units{% else %}does not reduce rule violation on held-out units{% endif %}. A related point is about what the fuzzy form costs. The rule base has no trained parameters, its breakpoints are cohort quantiles, and each explanation is a list of rule degrees an instructor can check against the student's transcript. That makes it easy to audit and also limits how much signal it can contribute: five expert rules should not be expected to match a regression fitted on 26 dialogue-act counts.

{% if S.e6_v_neg %}The direction check of Section 7.3 changes how the other results should be read. The fuzzy machinery worked as designed: memberships were fitted without leakage, the TSK output was graded, the implication loss was differentiable and the breakpoint grid behaved smoothly. What failed is the knowledge the rules encode. The most intuitive indicator of offloading, accepting generated code without a visible check, is associated with a smaller assisted–unaided gap in this course. A neuro-fuzzy method cannot correct a rule whose sign is wrong; it can only enforce it, or be trained to ignore it. Two consequences follow for soft-computing work in learning analytics, and Section 7.9 bears both out. First, expert rules should be checked against the outcome on training data, one antecedent at a time, before they are used as constraints, and this check is cheap. Second, when the check fails, a learned rule base whose consequents are fitted on training folds, with the expert rule base as its starting point, is a more defensible design than a fixed one. Section 7.8 runs that variant as a separately specified follow-up. Learning the consequents corrects the sign of FR1, but the rule score stays near chance, so on these data the limit lies in the rule inputs, not in how the rules are weighted.{% endif %}

The ablation shows that most of the logistic regression's signal reduces to two dialogue-act counts, and the cross-semester results of Section 7.9 point the same way: a model on the volume of a few kinds of turns transfers better than one on all eight. A simple model captures most of the available signal, which is also why CF-TSK can afford to be small.

Every feature depends on GPT-4.1 dialogue-act labels whose held-out agreement with humans is moderate (κ = 0.582 broad, 0.493 specific). This sets a ceiling on any model built on them, may explain why the broad-level aggregate model is competitive with models that consume noisier specific labels, and may partly explain why turn-order information adds less than expected.{% if S.e4_stable %} The fuzzy rule base is exposed to the same noise through its inputs, but the stability result of Section 7.4 suggests that graded memberships tolerate small shifts in a rate better than thresholds do, since a mislabelled turn moves a rate slightly rather than flipping a flag.{% else %} The fuzzy rule base is exposed to the same noise through its inputs, and Section 7.4 gives no evidence that graded memberships tolerate it better than thresholds.{% endif %} A replication with human-verified labels for a subset of turns, or with an ensemble of annotators to estimate per-label confidence, would separate the model's limits from the labels' limits.

# 9. Limitations and threats to validity
- Sample size and dependence. The 464 units come from 175 students, so a student's three units are not independent. Group-aware CV prevents leakage across folds, and the permutation test re-runs the grouped protocol, but neither creates independence. A larger or multi-institution corpus is the most direct remedy.
- Single course. All evidence comes from one upper-division AI course at one university over two semesters. The weak label needs unaided exams that follow assisted assignments on the same material; the dialogue-act schema and the rules were designed for programming work; and the AUC in the mid-0.60s is a single-course estimate. A convincing test would apply the unchanged pipeline to at least one course in another subject and institution and report per-course results.
- Weak-label noise. The assisted–unaided gap is also driven by exam difficulty, topic misalignment and ordinary variance; the source study's prior-performance baseline explains only R² 0.17 to 0.18 of the outcome, which caps achievable AUC.
- Hawthorne effect. Students knew their conversations were recorded, so estimated offloading is a lower bound on unmonitored behaviour.
- LLM-generated dialogue-act labels. Moderate held-out agreement propagates into every rate the rule base reads and into the two categories the ablation identifies as most predictive.
- Expert rules and breakpoints. The rule base encodes one author's reading of R1 to R3 and Table 1, and the consequent of FR2 (0.75) is a judgement. Section 7.4 tests the breakpoints but not alternative rule sets or consequents, and Section 7.3 shows that the antecedent of FR1 has the opposite sign to the one assumed; a learned variant in which consequents or breakpoints are tuned on training folds, ANFIS-style [22], is a natural next step but would trade some auditability for fit.
- Confirmation design. E10 and E11 score each semester once with models fitted only on the other, and their hypotheses were written down before they were run; E11 also keeps the input selection inside the training semester. Two threats remain. The follow-up analyses E7–E11 were designed after the expert rules had failed, so the choice to study volume-based, learned rules was informed by the same corpus. And the two cohorts come from one course at one university, with lower bounds in the reverse direction close to their thresholds. StudyChat currently contains only these two semesters. Scoring a later offering, or another course, with the frozen rule base released here is the test that would remove both threats.
- Construct validity. Offloading is operationalised through a behavioural and outcome proxy. The ablation gives indirect support, but the score is a decision-support signal, not a validated psychometric instrument.

# 10. Ethical considerations
An offloading-risk score used in a live course carries stakes beyond a benchmark: a false positive can stigmatise legitimate help-seeking, and a false negative gives false reassurance. We recommend showing the score and its rule explanation to the student first, as a prompt for self-reflection, before any instructor-facing use. StudyChat was collected under IRB approval with informed consent and PII filtering; this study is a secondary analysis of that public, de-identified dataset, and Saigon International University does not require separate ethics approval for such analyses. Researchers reusing the data remain bound by its CC BY 4.0 licence and by their institution's human-subjects rules if derived scores are applied to real students.

# 11. Conclusion
This paper asked whether fuzzy rules for cognitive-offloading risk in student–LLM dialogues should come from experts or from data, and whether either kind holds for a new cohort. On 464 units from 175 StudyChat students, a five-rule expert TSK rule base ranked units at chance (AUC {{S.f_auc}}), and as a Łukasiewicz implication constraint it left a Transformer's AUC unchanged (Δ = {{S.imp_d}}). Its main antecedent, unchecked acceptance of generated code, is inversely associated with the label (ρ = {{S.e6_v_rho}}). Learning the consequents on the same inputs did not rescue it (Section 7.8). A count-based rule base with eight grid rules and learned consequents did transfer. Trained on Fall 2024 and scored once on Spring 2025 it reached AUC {{S.tp_cf_tsk}}, non-inferior to logistic regression on all dialogue-act counts and statistically indistinguishable from a linear model on its own inputs, and the reverse direction gave {{S.tr_cf_tsk}}. Its rules mark low writing-request volume combined with pasted context as risk, in both directions. Fuzzy constraints added at most {{S.tr_h3.d}} AUC to the Transformer. The input choice was informed by pooled data, and both cohorts come from one course; a frozen-model test on a further offering is the next step before the rule table supports decisions about individual students.
